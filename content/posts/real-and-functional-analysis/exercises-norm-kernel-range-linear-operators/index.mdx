The exercises study linear operators between normed spaces, with $\lambda$ the Lebesgue measure on ${\mathbb{R}.}$

## Exercises

### The operator $Tf = \int_{-1}^{1} f g \, d\lambda$

**Example 14.1.** Prove that the map ${T \colon (C([-1, 1]), \|\cdot\|_\infty) \to (\mathbb{R}, |\cdot|)}$ given by

$$
Tf := \int_{-1}^{1} f g \, d\lambda, \qquad \text{where} \quad g(x) = \begin{cases} -1 & -1 \le x \le 0, \\ 1 & 0 < x \le 1, \end{cases}
$$

is linear and continuous.

For the operator $T$ of Example 14.1, let ${\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}}$ and ${f, h \in C([-1, 1]).}$ Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(\alpha f + \beta h) &= \int_{-1}^{1} (\alpha f + \beta h) g \, d\lambda \\
&= \int_{-1}^{1} (\alpha f g + \beta h g) \, d\lambda \\
&= \int_{-1}^{1} \alpha f g \, d\lambda + \int_{-1}^{1} \beta h g \, d\lambda \\
&= \alpha \int_{-1}^{1} f g \, d\lambda + \beta \int_{-1}^{1} h g \, d\lambda = \alpha Tf + \beta Th,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the third and the fourth equalities are the linearity of the integral, Remark 5.13. Hence $T$ is linear.

To prove that $T$ is continuous, we prove that it is bounded. For a linear operator $T$ from a normed space ${(X, \|\cdot\|_X)}$ to a normed space ${(Y, \|\cdot\|_Y)}$ this means, as in Definition 13.5 (ii), that there exists ${C > 0}$ such that ${\|Tf\|_Y \le C \|f\|_X}$ for every ${f \in X.}$ Here we need ${C > 0}$ such that ${|Tf| \le C \|f\|_\infty}$ for every ${f \in C([-1, 1]).}$ Let ${f \in C([-1, 1]).}$ Then, since ${|f| \le \|f\|_\infty}$ and ${|g| = 1,}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
|Tf| = \biggl| \int_{-1}^{1} f g \, d\lambda \biggr| &\le \int_{-1}^{1} |f| \, |g| \, d\lambda \\
&\le \|f\|_\infty \int_{-1}^{1} |g| \, d\lambda \\
&= 2 \|f\|_\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence $T$ is bounded, and $T$ is continuous by Theorem 13.8. We now compute ${\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}},}$ the norm of $T$ in ${\mathcal{L}(C([-1, 1]), \mathbb{R}).}$

**Remark 14.2.** Let ${(X, \|\cdot\|_X)}$ and ${(Y, \|\cdot\|_Y)}$ be normed spaces and let ${T \in \mathcal{L}(X, Y)}$ with ${T \neq 0.}$ By Proposition 13.10, $\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}}$ is the least constant ${C > 0}$ such that ${\|Tx\|_Y \le C \|x\|_X}$ for every ${x \in X.}$ In general, to compute ${\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}}{:}}$\
**(i)** try to estimate ${\|Tx\|_Y \le C \|x\|_X}$ for every ${x \in X,}$ keeping $C$ as small as possible;\
**(ii)** **(a)** find ${x_0 \in X}$ with ${x_0 \neq 0}$ such that ${\|Tx_0\|_Y = C \|x_0\|_X,}$ or\
**(b)** find ${(x_n) \subset X \setminus \{0\}}$ such that ${\|Tx_n\|_Y/\|x_n\|_X \to C.}$

For Example 14.1 we choose (b) of Remark 14.2. For every integer ${n \ge 1}$ let ${f_n \in C([-1, 1])}$ be equal to $-1$ on ${[-1, -1/n],}$ linear on $[-1/n, 1/n]$ and equal to $1$ on ${[1/n, 1],}$ as in Figure 14.1, so that ${\|f_n\|_\infty = 1.}$

<Ramp client:visible />

**Figure 14.1.** The drawing of Example 14.1: the function $f_n$ for the $n$ of the slider, equal to $-1$ on ${[-1, -1/n],}$ linear on $[-1/n, 1/n]$ and equal to $1$ on ${[1/n, 1].}$

Since ${f_n \, g = 1}$ on ${[-1, -1/n]}$ and on ${[1/n, 1],}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
|Tf_n| &= \biggl| \int_{-1}^{1} f_n \, g \, d\lambda \biggr| \\
&= \biggl| \int_{-1}^{-1/n} f_n \, g \, d\lambda + \int_{-1/n}^{1/n} f_n \, g \, d\lambda + \int_{1/n}^{1} f_n \, g \, d\lambda \biggr| \\
&= \biggl| -\frac{1}{n} + 1 + 1 - \frac{1}{n} + \int_{-1/n}^{1/n} f_n \, g \, d\lambda \biggr| \\
&\ge 2 - \biggl| -\frac{2}{n} + \int_{-1/n}^{1/n} f_n \, g \, d\lambda \biggr|.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here ${-2/n \to 0}$ and

$$
\biggl| \int_{-1/n}^{1/n} f_n \, g \, d\lambda \biggr| \le \|f_n\|_\infty \, \frac{2}{n} = \frac{2}{n} \longrightarrow 0.
$$

Hence

$$
\frac{|Tf_n|}{\|f_n\|_\infty} = |Tf_n| \ge 2 - \biggl| -\frac{2}{n} + \int_{-1/n}^{1/n} f_n \, g \, d\lambda \biggr| \xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} 2.
$$

In detail, ${|Tf_n| \le 2 \|f_n\|_\infty = 2}$ by the bound proved for the continuity of ${T,}$ so the ratio tends to ${2,}$ and ${\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}} = 2}$ by (b) of Remark 14.2.

### The operators $T_i f = \int_{-1}^{1} f \, x/(1 + x^2) \, d\lambda$

**Example 14.3.** Let ${X_1 = L^\infty((-1, 1))}$ and ${X_2 = C([-1, 1]),}$ and for ${i = 1, 2}$ let ${T_i \colon X_i \to X_i}$ be given by

$$
T_i f := \int_{-1}^{1} f \, \frac{x}{1 + x^2} \, d\lambda.
$$

**Note 14.4.** For every ${f \in X_i,}$ the image $T_i f$ is a constant function, and therefore ${\|T_i f\|_{X_i} = \|T_i f\|_\infty = |T_i f|.}$

(1) Is $T_i$ continuous? By Note 14.4, for every ${f \in X_i,}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|T_i f\|_\infty = |T_i f| &= \biggl| \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{x}{1 + x^2} \, f \, d\lambda \biggr| \\
&\le \|f\|_\infty \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{|x|}{1 + x^2} \, d\lambda \\
&= \|f\|_\infty \int_0^1 \frac{2x}{1 + x^2} \, d\lambda = (\log 2) \, \|f\|_\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence $T_i$ is bounded, hence continuous, for ${i = 1, 2.}$

(2) What is ${\|T_i\|_{\mathcal{L}(X_i, X_i)}?}$ For ${i = 1,}$ that is, ${X_1 = L^\infty((-1, 1)),}$ we choose

$$
f_0(x) = \begin{cases} -1 & -1 \le x \le 0, \\ 1 & 0 < x \le 1, \end{cases}
$$

which belongs to ${L^\infty((-1, 1))}$ and satisfies ${\|f_0\|_\infty = 1.}$ Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\|T_1 f_0\|_\infty}{\|f_0\|_\infty} &= \|T_1 f_0\|_\infty = |T_1 f_0| \\
&= \biggl| \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{x}{1 + x^2} \, f_0 \, d\lambda \biggr| \\
&= \biggl| \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{|x|}{1 + x^2} \, d\lambda \biggr| \\
&= \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{|x|}{1 + x^2} \, d\lambda = \log 2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence, by (1) and (a) of Remark 14.2, ${\|T_1\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^\infty, L^\infty)} = \log 2.}$ For ${i = 2,}$ that is, ${X_2 = C([-1, 1]),}$ we take the functions $f_n$ of Figure 14.1, with ${\|f_n\|_\infty = 1.}$ Then

$$
\frac{\|T_2 f_n\|_\infty}{\|f_n\|_\infty} = |T_2 f_n| = \biggl| \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{x}{1 + x^2} \, f_n \, d\lambda \biggr|.
$$

Moreover ${f_n \to H}$ in ${L^1((-1, 1)),}$ where

$$
H(x) = \begin{cases} -1 & -1 \le x < 0, \\ 1 & 0 < x < 1. \end{cases}
$$

Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{n \to \infty} |T_2 f_n| &= \biggl| \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{x}{1 + x^2} \, H \, d\lambda \biggr| \\
&= \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{|x|}{1 + x^2} \, d\lambda = \log 2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Indeed, ${|x|/(1 + x^2) \le 1}$ on ${(-1, 1),}$ so the integrals of ${x f_n/(1 + x^2)}$ and of ${x H/(1 + x^2)}$ differ at most by ${\|f_n - H\|_1,}$ which tends to ${0.}$ Hence, by (1) and (b) of Remark 14.2, ${\|T_2\|_{\mathcal{L}(C, C)} = \log 2.}$

(3) Let ${g(x) = x^2}$ for ${x \in (-1, 1).}$ Is ${g \in \operatorname{Ker}(T_1)?}$ Is $T_1$ injective? We compute

$$
T_1 g = \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{x}{1 + x^2} \, x^2 \, d\lambda = \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{x^3}{1 + x^2} \, d\lambda = 0,
$$

because the function ${x^3/(1 + x^2)}$ is odd. Hence ${g \in \operatorname{Ker}(T_1).}$

**Remark 14.5.** Let $T$ be a linear operator between normed spaces. Then $T$ is injective if and only if ${\operatorname{Ker}(T) = \{0\}.}$

By Remark 14.5, since ${g \neq 0,}$ the operator $T_1$ is not injective.

### The operator $(Tf)(x) = e^{-x} \int_0^x e^y \, f(y) \, dy$

**Example 14.6.** Let ${T \colon L^\infty((0, 1)) \to L^\infty((0, 1))}$ be given by

$$
(Tf)(x) := e^{-x} \int_0^x e^y \, f(y) \, dy \qquad \text{for every } f \in L^\infty((0, 1)) \text{ and every } x \in (0, 1).
$$

**(1)** Is $T$ continuous?

For the operator $T$ of Example 14.6, let ${f \in L^\infty((0, 1))}$ and ${x \in (0, 1).}$ Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
|(Tf)(x)| &= e^{-x} \biggl| \int_0^x e^y \, f(y) \, dy \biggr| \\
&\le e^{-x} \int_0^x e^y \, |f(y)| \, dy \\
&\le \|f\|_\infty \, e^{-x} \int_0^x e^y \, dy \\
&= \|f\|_\infty (1 - e^{-x}) \le (1 - e^{-1}) \|f\|_\infty,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the integral of $e^y$ over $(0, x)$ is ${e^x - 1,}$ and ${e^{-x} \ge e^{-1}.}$ Hence ${\|Tf\|_\infty \le (1 - e^{-1}) \|f\|_\infty}$ for every ${f \in L^\infty((0, 1)),}$ and $T$ is continuous.

(2) What is ${\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}}?}$ We choose ${f_0 = 1,}$ so that ${\|f_0\|_\infty = 1.}$ Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\|Tf_0\|_\infty}{\|f_0\|_\infty} = \|Tf_0\|_\infty &= \biggl\| e^{-x} \int_0^x e^y \, dy \biggr\|_\infty \\
&= \|1 - e^{-x}\|_\infty = 1 - e^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For the last equality, Figure 14.2 draws the graphs of ${e^{-x},}$ which decreases from ${1,}$ and of ${1 - e^{-x},}$ which increases from ${0.}$ Hence, by (1) and (a) of Remark 14.2, ${\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}} = 1 - e^{-1}.}$

<Exponentials />

**Figure 14.2.** The drawings of (2) in Example 14.6: on the left the graph of ${e^{-x},}$ on the right the graph of ${1 - e^{-x},}$ for ${x \in [0, 1].}$

(3) Is $T$ injective? The idea is to impose ${f \in \operatorname{Ker}(T),}$ that is, ${Tf = 0,}$ and to look for necessary conditions on ${f.}$ For ${f \in L^\infty((0, 1))}$ let

$$
F(x) := \int_0^x e^y \, f(y) \, dy \qquad \text{for } x \in [0, 1],
$$

so that ${(Tf)(x) = e^{-x} \, F(x)}$ for ${x \in (0, 1).}$ Let ${f \in \operatorname{Ker}(T).}$ Then ${e^{-x} \, F(x) = 0}$ for almost every ${x \in (0, 1).}$

In fact, ${e^{-x} \, F(x) = 0}$ for every ${x \in (0, 1).}$ The function ${y \mapsto e^y \, f(y)}$ belongs to ${L^\infty((0, 1)) \subset L^1((0, 1)),}$ so $F$ is continuous by the first fundamental theorem of calculus, Theorem 9.9. Hence $Tf$ is continuous, and it vanishes at every point because ${f \in \operatorname{Ker}(T).}$ Indeed, a continuous function that vanishes almost everywhere on $(0, 1)$ vanishes at every point of ${(0, 1),}$ since every nonempty open interval has positive measure.

Since ${e^{-x} > 0,}$ it follows that ${F(x) = 0}$ for almost every ${x \in (0, 1).}$ Moreover, since ${y \mapsto e^y \, f(y)}$ belongs to ${L^1((0, 1)),}$ the function $F$ belongs to ${\mathrm{AC}([0, 1]),}$ and ${F'(x)}$ exists and equals ${e^x \, f(x)}$ for almost every ${x \in (0, 1).}$ In detail, $F$ vanishes at every point of ${(0, 1),}$ because $Tf$ does, so ${F' = 0}$ on ${(0, 1).}$ Hence ${0 = e^x \, f(x)}$ for almost every ${x,}$ where ${e^x > 0.}$ So ${f(x) = 0}$ for almost every ${x,}$ that is, ${f = 0}$ in ${L^\infty((0, 1)).}$ Therefore ${\operatorname{Ker}(T) = \{0\},}$ and $T$ is injective by Remark 14.5.

(4) Is $T$ surjective? The idea is to look for necessary conditions on $Tf$ as $f$ varies in ${L^\infty((0, 1)).}$

(i) Let ${f \in L^\infty((0, 1)).}$ In ${(Tf)(x) = e^{-x} \, F(x)}$ the factor $e^{-x}$ is continuous, and ${F \in C([0, 1])}$ by the first fundamental theorem of calculus, because $e^y \, f(y)$ belongs to ${L^1((0, 1)).}$ Hence ${Tf \in C([0, 1])}$ for every ${f \in L^\infty((0, 1)).}$ Therefore ${R(T) \subset C([0, 1]) \subsetneq L^\infty((0, 1)),}$ and $T$ is not surjective.

(ii) We can observe that ${R(T) \subset \mathrm{AC}([0, 1]).}$ Both factors of ${(Tf)(x) = e^{-x} \, F(x)}$ belong to ${\mathrm{AC}([0, 1]){:}}$ the function ${e^{-x},}$ and the function $F$ of (3).

**Remark 14.7.** Let ${a < b}$ be real numbers, let ${v_1, v_2 \in \mathrm{AC}([a, b])}$ and let ${v := v_1 \, v_2.}$ Then ${v \in \mathrm{AC}([a, b]).}$

**Proof (complete).** Let ${\varepsilon > 0.}$ By Definition 9.11, applied to $v_1$ and to $v_2$ with ${\varepsilon/(1 + \|v_1\|_\infty + \|v_2\|_\infty)}$ in place of ${\varepsilon,}$ for ${i = 1, 2}$ there exists ${\delta_i > 0}$ such that, for every finite family ${\{ (a_j, b_j) : j = 1, \dots, N \}}$ of pairwise disjoint subintervals of ${[a, b],}$

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{N} (b_j - a_j) < \delta_i \implies \sum_{j=1}^{N} |v_i(b_j) - v_i(a_j)| < \frac{\varepsilon}{1 + \|v_1\|_\infty + \|v_2\|_\infty}.
$$

We take ${\delta = \min\{\delta_1, \delta_2\}}$ and estimate. If ${(b_1 - a_1) + \dots + (b_N - a_N) < \delta,}$ then

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\sum_{j=1}^{N} |v(b_j) - v(a_j)| = \sum_{j=1}^{N} |v_1(b_j) v_2(b_j) - v_1(a_j) v_2(a_j)| \\
&\quad \le \sum_{j=1}^{N} \bigl( |v_1(b_j)| \, |v_2(b_j) - v_2(a_j)| + |v_2(a_j)| \, |v_1(b_j) - v_1(a_j)| \bigr) \\
&\quad \le \|v_1\|_\infty \sum_{j=1}^{N} |v_2(b_j) - v_2(a_j)| + \|v_2\|_\infty \sum_{j=1}^{N} |v_1(b_j) - v_1(a_j)| \\
&\quad \le \frac{\varepsilon}{1 + \|v_1\|_\infty + \|v_2\|_\infty} \bigl( \|v_1\|_\infty + \|v_2\|_\infty \bigr) < \varepsilon.
\end{aligned}
$$

In the first inequality, ${v_1(b_j) v_2(a_j)}$ is added and subtracted inside each absolute value. The second uses ${|v_1(b_j)| \le \|v_1\|_\infty}$ and ${|v_2(a_j)| \le \|v_2\|_\infty,}$ and the third the choice of ${\delta.}$ The last inequality holds because ${\|v_1\|_\infty + \|v_2\|_\infty < 1 + \|v_1\|_\infty + \|v_2\|_\infty.}$ Hence ${v \in \mathrm{AC}([a, b]).}$ $\blacksquare$

By Remark 14.7, applied on $[0, 1]$ to the functions $e^{-x}$ and ${F,}$ the function $Tf$ belongs to ${\mathrm{AC}([0, 1])}$ for every ${f \in L^\infty((0, 1)).}$ That is, ${R(T) \subset \mathrm{AC}([0, 1]).}$

### The operator $(Tf)(x) = (2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(x)) f(x + 1)$

**Example 14.8.** Let ${T \colon L^1((0, \infty)) \to L^1((0, \infty))}$ be given by

$$
(Tf)(x) := \big( 2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(x) \big) f(x + 1) \qquad \text{for almost every } x > 0.
$$

**(1)** Is $T$ continuous?

For the operator $T$ of Example 14.8 and every ${f \in L^1((0, \infty)),}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|Tf\|_1 = \int_0^\infty |Tf| \, d\lambda &= \int_0^\infty \big( 2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(x) \big) \, |f(x + 1)| \, d\lambda \\
&= \int_0^\infty 2 \, |f(x + 1)| \, d\lambda + \int_1^2 |f(x + 1)| \, d\lambda \\
&= 2 \int_1^\infty |f(x)| \, d\lambda + \int_2^3 |f(x)| \, d\lambda \\
&\le 3 \int_0^\infty |f| \, d\lambda = 3 \|f\|_1.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence $T$ is bounded, hence continuous.

(2) What is ${\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}}?}$ Let ${f_0 \in L^1((0, \infty))}$ be a function with ${f_0 \neq 0}$ that vanishes outside ${[2, 3],}$ as in Figure 14.3.

<Bump a={2} b={3} end={4.4} />

**Figure 14.3.** The drawing of (2) in Example 14.8, an instance: a function $f_0$ of ${L^1((0, \infty))}$ with ${f_0 \neq 0,}$ equal to 0 outside ${[2, 3],}$ with the parts where it is 0 thick on the axis.

By the computation of (1),

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|Tf_0\|_1 &= 2 \int_1^\infty |f_0| \, d\lambda + \int_2^3 |f_0| \, d\lambda \\
&= 2 \int_2^3 |f_0| \, d\lambda + \int_2^3 |f_0| \, d\lambda \\
&= 3 \int_2^3 |f_0| \, d\lambda = 3 \int_0^\infty |f_0| \, d\lambda = 3 \|f_0\|_1.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence, by (1) and (a) of Remark 14.2, ${\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}} = 3.}$

(3) Is $T$ injective? Let ${f \in \operatorname{Ker}(T),}$ that is, ${Tf = 0.}$ Then ${(2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(x)) f(x + 1) = 0}$ for almost every ${x > 0,}$ where ${2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(x) > 0.}$ Hence ${f(x + 1) = 0}$ for almost every ${x > 0.}$ That is, ${f(y) = 0}$ for almost every ${y > 1.}$ Then we take a function ${f_0 \in L^1((0, \infty))}$ with ${f_0 \neq 0}$ that vanishes on ${(1, \infty),}$ as in Figure 14.4.

<Bump a={0} b={1} end={3.4} dots />

**Figure 14.4.** The drawing of (3) in Example 14.8, an instance: a function $f_0$ of ${L^1((0, \infty))}$ with ${f_0 \neq 0,}$ equal to 0 on ${(1, \infty),}$ where it is thick on the axis.

Then ${(Tf_0)(x) = (2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(x)) f_0(x + 1)}$ for almost every ${x > 0.}$ Notice that ${f_0(x + 1) = 0}$ for every ${x > 0.}$ Hence ${(Tf_0)(x) = 0}$ for almost every ${x,}$ that is, ${Tf_0 = 0}$ in ${L^1((0, \infty)).}$ So ${f_0 \in \operatorname{Ker}(T)}$ and ${f_0 \neq 0,}$ and $T$ is not injective.

(4) Is $T$ surjective? Let ${u \in R(T),}$ so that there exists ${f \in L^1((0, \infty))}$ with ${Tf = u.}$ Then ${u(x) = (2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(x)) f(x + 1)}$ for almost every ${x > 0,}$ and, since ${2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(x) > 0,}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x + 1) &= \frac{u(x)}{2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(x)} && \text{for almost every } x > 0, \\
f(y) &= \frac{u(y - 1)}{2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(y - 1)} && \text{for almost every } y > 1,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the second line is the first with ${y = x + 1.}$ Then, if ${u \in L^1((0, \infty)),}$ consider

$$
f(y) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{for almost every } y \in (0, 1), \\[4pt] \dfrac{u(y - 1)}{2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(y - 1)} & \text{for almost every } y > 1. \end{cases}
$$

(i) ${f \in L^1((0, \infty)).}$ Indeed, since ${2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(y - 1) \ge 2,}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\infty |f| \, d\lambda &= \int_1^\infty \frac{|u(y - 1)|}{2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(y - 1)} \, d\lambda \\
&\le \frac{1}{2} \int_1^\infty |u(y - 1)| \, d\lambda \\
&= \frac{1}{2} \int_0^\infty |u| \, d\lambda < +\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

(ii) For almost every ${x > 0,}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
(Tf)(x) &= \big( 2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(x) \big) f(x + 1) \\
&= \big( 2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(x) \big) \frac{u(x + 1 - 1)}{2 + \chi_{[1, 2]}(x + 1 - 1)} \\
&= u(x).
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence for every ${u \in L^1((0, \infty))}$ there exists ${f \in L^1((0, \infty))}$ such that ${Tf = u,}$ and $T$ is surjective.

### The operator $(Tx)^{(k)} = k x^{(k)}/(1 + k^2)$

**Example 14.9.** Let ${T \colon \ell^2 \to \ell^2}$ be given by

$$
(Tx)^{(k)} = \frac{k}{1 + k^2} \, x^{(k)} \qquad \text{for every } x \in \ell^2 \text{ and every } k \in \mathbb{N}, \ k \ge 0.
$$

**(1)** Is $T$ continuous?

For the operator $T$ of Example 14.9 and every ${x \in \ell^2,}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|Tx\|_2^2 &= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \Big( \frac{k}{1 + k^2} \, x^{(k)} \Big)^2 \\
&= \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \Big( \frac{k}{1 + k^2} \Big)^2 \bigl( x^{(k)} \bigr)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Note 14.10.** For ${k = 0}$ the factor ${k/(1 + k^2)}$ equals ${0.}$

By Note 14.10, only the indices ${k \ge 1}$ count, and we check that ${k/(1 + k^2) \le 1/2}$ for every ${k \ge 1.}$ This holds if and only if ${2k \le 1 + k^2,}$ that is, ${0 \le k^2 - 2k + 1 = (k - 1)^2,}$ which is true. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|Tx\|_2^2 &= \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \Big( \frac{k}{1 + k^2} \Big)^2 \bigl( x^{(k)} \bigr)^2 \\
&\le \frac{1}{4} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \bigl( x^{(k)} \bigr)^2 \\
&\le \frac{1}{4} \|x\|_2^2,
\end{aligned}
$$

that is, ${\|Tx\|_2 \le \frac{1}{2} \|x\|_2}$ for every ${x \in \ell^2,}$ and $T$ is continuous.

(2) What is ${\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}}?}$ Let ${x_0 = (0, 1, 0, 0, \dots) \in \ell^2,}$ so that ${\|x_0\|_2 = 1.}$ Since ${x_0^{(k)} \neq 0}$ only for ${k = 1,}$ where ${x_0^{(1)} = 1,}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\|Tx_0\|_2}{\|x_0\|_2} = \|Tx_0\|_2 &= \biggl( \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \Big( \frac{k}{1 + k^2} \Big)^2 \bigl( x_0^{(k)} \bigr)^2 \biggr)^{\frac{1}{2}} \\
&= \biggl( \Big( \frac{1}{1 + 1} \Big)^2 \bigl( x_0^{(1)} \bigr)^2 \biggr)^{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{1}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence, by (1) and (a) of Remark 14.2, ${\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}} = \frac{1}{2}.}$
