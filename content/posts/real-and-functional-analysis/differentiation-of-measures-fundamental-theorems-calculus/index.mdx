A measure can be obtained from another measure by integrating a function, and a function on an interval of $\mathbb{R}$ can sometimes be obtained by integrating its derivative. In both cases the question is when this happens.

## Differentiation of measures

**Remark 1.** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space and let ${f \colon X \to [0, +\infty)}$ be measurable. Then ${\nu = f\mu,}$ defined by

$$
\nu(E) := \int_E f \, d\mu \qquad \text{for every } E \in \mathcal{M},
$$

is another measure on ${(X, \mathcal{M}),}$ by Proposition 8 of the sixth post with $f$ in place of ${\varphi.}$ Figure 1 draws $\nu(E)$ for an instance.

**Figure 1.** An instance with $X$ an interval and ${\mu = \lambda{:}}$ the graph of ${f,}$ a set $E$ on the axis, and the region under the graph over ${E,}$ whose area is ${\nu(E).}$

The question raised by Remark 1 is the converse. Given two measures $\nu$ and $\mu$ on ${(X, \mathcal{M}),}$ does there exist $f$ such that ${\nu = f\mu?}$

**Definition 2 (Absolutely continuous measure).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M})}$ be a measurable space and let ${\mu, \nu \colon \mathcal{M} \to [0, +\infty]}$ be two measures. We say that $\nu$ is *absolutely continuous* with respect to ${\mu,}$ and we write ${\nu \ll \mu,}$ if

$$
\mu(E) = 0 \implies \nu(E) = 0 \qquad \text{for every } E \in \mathcal{M}.
$$

**Example 3.** **(i)** Let ${\mu, \nu \colon \mathcal{M} \to [0, +\infty]}$ be two measures on a measurable space ${(X, \mathcal{M})}$ such that ${\nu = f\mu}$ for some measurable ${f \colon X \to [0, +\infty).}$ Then ${\nu \ll \mu{:}}$ if ${\mu(E) = 0,}$ then ${\nu(E) = \int_E f \, d\mu = 0}$ by (1) of Remark 4 of the fifth post.\
**(ii)** On ${(\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R})),}$ consider the Lebesgue measure $\lambda$ and the Dirac delta $\delta_0$ at zero of Example 4 of the second post. Then ${\lambda \not\ll \delta_0.}$ The set ${E = [1, 2]}$ does not contain ${0,}$ so that ${\delta_0(E) = 0,}$ but ${\lambda(E) = 1 \neq 0,}$ as in Figure 2.

**Theorem 4 (Radon–Nikodym).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M})}$ be a measurable space and let ${\mu, \nu \colon \mathcal{M} \to [0, +\infty]}$ be two measures. Suppose that ${\nu \ll \mu}$ and that $\mu$ and $\nu$ are $\sigma$-finite. Then there exists a measurable function ${f \colon X \to [0, +\infty)}$ such that ${\nu = f\mu.}$

In Theorem 4, the measure $\mu$ is $\sigma$-finite as in Definition 8 (iii) of the second post. That is, there exists ${(E_m) \subset \mathcal{M}}$ with ${\bigcup_m E_m = X}$ and ${\mu(E_m) < +\infty}$ for every ${m.}$

A function $f$ as in Theorem 4 is called a *Radon–Nikodym derivative* of $\nu$ with respect to ${\mu.}$ Theorem 4 says that ${d\nu = f \, d\mu,}$ which suggests the notation ${f = d\nu/d\mu.}$

### The counting measure on $[0, 1]$

**Remark 5.** In Theorem 4 the assumption that $\mu$ is $\sigma$-finite is needed. Otherwise, on ${([0, 1], \mathcal{L}([0, 1])),}$ take the Lebesgue measure $\lambda$ and the counting measure $\mu$ on ${[0, 1]}$ of Example 3 of the second post, that is, ${\mu(E) = \#E}$ when $E$ is finite and ${\mu(E) = +\infty}$ otherwise. Then $\mu$ is not $\sigma$-finite, ${\lambda \ll \mu,}$ and there exists no measurable ${f \colon [0, 1] \to [0, +\infty)}$ such that ${\lambda = f\mu.}$

The measure $\mu$ is not $\sigma$-finite on ${[0, 1],}$ because ${\mu(E) < +\infty}$ if and only if $E$ is finite. If there existed ${(E_m) \subset \mathcal{L}([0, 1])}$ with ${\mu(E_m) < +\infty}$ for every $m$ and ${[0, 1] = \bigcup_m E_m,}$ then ${[0, 1]}$ would be countable, which is impossible.

Moreover ${\lambda \ll \mu.}$ If ${\mu(E) = 0,}$ then ${E = \emptyset,}$ and ${\lambda(\emptyset) = 0.}$ Finally, suppose that a measurable ${f \colon [0, 1] \to [0, +\infty)}$ satisfies ${\lambda = f\mu.}$ Then

$$
1 = \lambda([0, 1]) = \int_{[0, 1]} f \, d\mu,
$$

hence there exists ${E \in \mathcal{L}([0, 1])}$ such that ${\mu(E) > 0}$ and ${f > 0}$ on ${E.}$ Indeed, the set ${E = \{ f > 0 \}}$ works, since otherwise ${\{ f > 0 \} = \emptyset}$ and ${\int_{[0, 1]} f \, d\mu = 0.}$ Up to passing to a subset, ${0 < \mu(E) < +\infty,}$ as in Figure 3, hence $E$ is finite and ${\lambda(E) = 0.}$ Therefore

$$
0 = \lambda(E) = \int_E f \, d\mu > 0,
$$

which is impossible. In detail, the last inequality is (1) of Corollary 10 of the fifth post, which applies because only $\emptyset$ has measure zero, and ${f > 0}$ on $E$ with ${\mu(E) > 0.}$

### Lebesgue differentiation theorem

**Theorem 6 (Lebesgue differentiation theorem).** Let ${f \in \mathcal{L}^1(\mathbb{R}^N, \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^N), \lambda).}$ Then, for almost every ${x \in \mathbb{R}^N,}$

$$
\lim_{r \to 0^+} \frac{1}{\lambda(B_r(x))} \int_{B_r(x)} |f(\cdot) - f(x)| \, d\lambda = 0,
$$

where $B_r(x)$ is the open ball of centre $x$ and radius ${r.}$ In the integral, ${f(\cdot)}$ varies, while ${f(x)}$ is a constant. In particular, if ${N = 1,}$ then ${B_r(x) = (x - r, x + r)}$ and ${\lambda(B_r(x)) = 2r,}$ so that, as in Figure 4 for an instance,

$$
\lim_{r \to 0^+} \frac{1}{2r} \int_{x-r}^{x+r} |f - f(x)| \, d\lambda = 0 \qquad \text{for almost every } x \in \mathbb{R}.
$$

## Fundamental theorems of calculus

For continuous functions the fundamental theorem of calculus is already known.

**Theorem 7 (Baby fundamental theorem of calculus).** Let ${a < b}$ be real numbers, let ${f \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}}$ be continuous, and define

$$
F(x) = \int_a^x f(t) \, dt, \qquad x \in [a, b].
$$

Then $F$ is continuous on ${[a, b],}$ and $F$ is differentiable at every ${x \in [a, b],}$ with ${F'(x) = f(x),}$ as in Figure 5. Moreover, ${F(y) - F(x) = \int_x^y f(t) \, dt}$ for all ${x, y \in [a, b]}$ with ${x < y.}$

**Corollary 8.** Let ${a < b}$ be real numbers and let ${g \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}}$ belong to ${C^1([a, b]),}$ the set of the continuously differentiable functions on ${[a, b].}$ Then

$$
g(x) = g(a) + \int_a^x g'(t) \, dt \qquad \text{for every } x \in [a, b].
$$

<Baby client:visible />

**Figure 5.** An instance of Theorem 7: above, a continuous function $f$ on ${[a, b]}$ and the region under its graph over ${[a, x],}$ whose area is ${F(x);}$ below, the graph of $F$ and its tangent at ${x,}$ of slope ${f(x).}$ The slider sets ${x.}$

The question is what can be proved, for ${f \in L^1,}$ about the function

$$
F(x) = \int_a^x f \, d\lambda.
$$

**Theorem 9 (First fundamental theorem of calculus).** Let ${-\infty < a < b < +\infty,}$ let ${f \in L^1((a, b)),}$ and let

$$
F(x) := \int_a^x f \, d\lambda \qquad \text{for every } x \in [a, b].
$$

Then $F$ is continuous on ${[a, b],}$ $F$ is differentiable almost everywhere on ${(a, b),}$ and ${F'(x) = f(x)}$ at almost every ${x \in (a, b).}$ Moreover, ${F(y) - F(x) = \int_x^y f \, d\lambda}$ for all ${x, y \in [a, b]}$ with ${x < y.}$

**Proof (sketch).** In the proof of differentiability only the case ${h > 0}$ is treated. First, let ${(x_n) \subset [a, b]}$ with ${x_n \to x_0 \in [a, b].}$ We need to prove that ${F(x_n) \to F(x_0).}$ We have

$$
F(x_n) = \int_a^{x_n} f \, d\lambda = \int_a^b f \chi_{(a, x_n)} \, d\lambda,
$$

where ${f \chi_{(a, x_n)} \to f \chi_{(a, x_0)}}$ almost everywhere as ${n \to \infty,}$ and ${|f \chi_{(a, x_n)}| \le |f| \in L^1((a, b))}$ for every ${n,}$ as in Figure 6. By the dominated convergence theorem, Theorem 10 of the sixth post, ${f \chi_{(a, x_n)} \to f \chi_{(a, x_0)}}$ in ${L^1((a, b)).}$ In detail, ${\chi_{(a, x_n)}(t) \to \chi_{(a, x_0)}(t)}$ at every ${t \neq x_0,}$ and the convergence in $L^1$ gives the convergence of the integrals by (2) of Remark 12 of the fifth post. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_n F(x_n) &= \lim_n \int_a^b f \chi_{(a, x_n)} \, d\lambda \\
&= \int_a^b f \chi_{(a, x_0)} \, d\lambda = \int_a^{x_0} f \, d\lambda \\
&= F(x_0).
\end{aligned}
$$

**Figure 6.** An instance of the proof of continuity: the dashed graph of ${|f|,}$ the graph of ${f \chi_{(a, x_n)},}$ equal to $f$ on ${(a, x_n)}$ and to 0 after ${x_n,}$ and, shaded, the region where it differs from ${f \chi_{(a, x_0)},}$ between $x_0$ and ${x_n.}$ The slider sets ${n.}$

Next, choose a representative ${f \in \mathcal{L}^1((a, b))}$ of the class of ${f.}$ By Theorem 6 with ${N = 1,}$ at almost every ${x \in (a, b),}$

$$
\lim_{r \to 0^+} \frac{1}{2r} \int_{x-r}^{x+r} |f - f(x)| \, d\lambda = 0.
$$

Indeed, Theorem 6 applies to the function equal to $f$ on ${(a, b)}$ and to 0 elsewhere, which belongs to ${\mathcal{L}^1(\mathbb{R}),}$ and for small $r$ the interval ${(x - r, x + r)}$ lies in ${(a, b).}$ Now fix ${x \in (a, b)}$ such that this limit holds: such points are called *Lebesgue points* of ${f.}$ We want to prove that

$$
\Big| \frac{F(x + h) - F(x)}{h} - f(x) \Big| \longrightarrow 0 \qquad \text{as } h \to 0.
$$

For ${h > 0}$ so small that ${(x - h, x + h) \subset (a, b),}$ we compute, as in Figure 7,

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 \le \left| \frac{F(x + h) - F(x)}{h} - f(x) \right| &= \left| \frac{1}{h} \Big( \int_a^{x+h} f \, d\lambda - \int_a^x f \, d\lambda \Big) - f(x) \right| \\
&= \left| \frac{1}{h} \int_x^{x+h} f \, d\lambda - f(x) \right| \\
&= \left| \frac{1}{h} \int_x^{x+h} f \, d\lambda - \frac{1}{h} \int_x^{x+h} f(x) \, d\lambda \right| \\
&= \frac{1}{h} \left| \int_x^{x+h} \big( f - f(x) \big) \, d\lambda \right| \\
&\le \frac{1}{h} \int_x^{x+h} |f - f(x)| \, d\lambda \le \frac{2}{2h} \int_{x-h}^{x+h} |f - f(x)| \, d\lambda.
\end{aligned}
$$

The third equality uses that ${\alpha = (1/h) \int_x^{x+h} \alpha \, d\lambda}$ for every ${\alpha \in \mathbb{R},}$ with ${\alpha = f(x).}$ In detail, the second equality holds because ${(a, x + h)}$ is the disjoint union of ${(a, x),}$ $\{x\}$ and ${(x, x + h),}$ with ${\lambda(\{x\}) = 0,}$ and the last inequality because ${(x, x + h) \subset (x - h, x + h).}$ As ${h \to 0,}$ the last term tends to $0$ because $x$ is a Lebesgue point. Hence, at almost every ${x \in (a, b),}$

$$
\lim_{h \to 0} \Big| \frac{F(x + h) - F(x)}{h} - f(x) \Big| = 0, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad F'(x) = f(x). \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

### The Cantor and Heaviside functions

**Remark 10.** Let ${g \colon (a, b) \to \mathbb{R}}$ be such that $g'(x)$ exists for almost every ${x \in (a, b)}$ and ${g' \in L^1((a, b)).}$ Does it follow that “${g(x) = \int_a^x g' \, d\lambda}$”, in the sense of Corollary 8, that is, ${g(y) - g(x) = \int_x^y g' \, d\lambda}$ for ${x < y?}$ No, as (1) and (2) show.

**(1)** Let ${v \colon [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}}$ be the Cantor function of Example 8 of the third post. Then $v'(x)$ exists and ${v'(x) = 0}$ for almost every ${x,}$ by Example 9 (iii) of the fourth post, and ${v' = 0 \in L^1((0, 1)).}$ But the formula fails for ${x = 0}$ and ${y = 1,}$ as in Figure 8:

$$
v(1) - v(0) = 1 \neq \int_0^1 v' \, d\lambda = \int_0^1 0 \, d\lambda = 0.
$$

**(2)** Let ${H \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}}$ be the Heaviside function, equal to 0 on ${(-\infty, 0]}$ and to 1 on ${(0, +\infty),}$ as in Figure 9. Then $H'(x)$ exists and ${H'(x) = 0}$ for every ${x \neq 0,}$ but, for every ${x > 0,}$

$$
\int_{-3}^x H' \, d\lambda = 0 \neq H(x) - H(-3).
$$

### Absolutely continuous functions

**Definition 11 (Absolutely continuous function).** Let ${a < b}$ be real numbers and ${f \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}.}$ We say that $f$ is *absolutely continuous* on ${[a, b]}$ if for every positive $\varepsilon$ there exists a positive $\delta$ such that, for every finite family ${\{ (a_i, b_i) : i = 1, \dots, m \}}$ of pairwise disjoint subintervals of ${[a, b],}$

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m} (b_i - a_i) < \delta \qquad \text{implies} \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{m} |f(b_i) - f(a_i)| < \varepsilon.
$$

Here the intervals are numbered so that ${a \le a_1 < b_1 \le a_2 < b_2 \le \dots < b_m \le b,}$ as in Figure 10, and we write ${f \in \mathrm{AC}([a, b]).}$

**Remark 12 (Lipschitz and uniformly continuous functions).** Let ${a < b}$ be real numbers and ${f \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}.}$ The function $f$ is *Lipschitz* on ${[a, b]}$ if there exists ${L > 0}$ such that ${|f(y) - f(x)| \le L |y - x|}$ for all ${x, y \in [a, b].}$ It is *uniformly continuous* on ${[a, b]}$ if for every positive $\varepsilon$ there exists a positive $\delta$ such that, for all ${x < y}$ in ${[a, b],}$ ${|y - x| < \delta}$ implies ${|f(y) - f(x)| < \varepsilon.}$ In this case we write ${f \in \mathrm{UC}([a, b]).}$ Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
f \text{ Lipschitz on } [a, b] &\implies f \in \mathrm{AC}([a, b]) \\
&\implies f \in \mathrm{UC}([a, b]) \implies f \text{ continuous on } [a, b].
\end{aligned}
$$

**Lemma 13 (Absolute continuity of the integral).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space and let ${f \in L^1.}$ Then for every positive $\varepsilon$ there exists a positive $\delta$ such that, for every ${E \in \mathcal{M}}$ with ${\mu(E) < \delta,}$

$$
\int_E |f| \, d\mu < \varepsilon.
$$

**Proof (complete).** Let ${\varepsilon > 0.}$ Take a function $s$ such that (1) $s$ is simple, (2) ${0 \le s \le |f|}$ almost everywhere, and (3) ${\int_X s \, d\mu \ge \int_X |f| \, d\mu - \varepsilon/2.}$ Indeed, such an $s$ exists by Definition 3 of the fifth post, because ${\int_X |f| \, d\mu < +\infty,}$ and it can be taken with ${s \le |f|}$ at every point.

Let ${M := \max_X s.}$ We can take ${\delta := \varepsilon/(2M).}$ In detail, $M$ is a real number, because $s$ takes finitely many values and is at most $|f|$ at every point, and if ${M = 0}$ every ${\delta > 0}$ works, because (3) then gives ${\int_X |f| \, d\mu \le \varepsilon/2.}$ So take ${E \in \mathcal{M}}$ with ${\mu(E) < \delta.}$ Then, as in Figure 11,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_E |f| \, d\mu &= \int_E \big( |f| - s \big) \, d\mu + \int_E s \, d\mu \\
&\le \int_X \big( |f| - s \big) \, d\mu + M \mu(E) \\
&< \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + M \frac{\varepsilon}{2M} \\
&= \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} = \varepsilon,
\end{aligned}
$$

where ${|f| - s \ge 0,}$ the first inequality is monotonicity, in the domain of integration and, as ${s \le M,}$ in the function, and the second uses (3) and ${\mu(E) < \delta = \varepsilon/(2M).}$ $\blacksquare$

### Second fundamental theorem of calculus

**Theorem 14 (Second fundamental theorem of calculus).** Let ${a < b}$ be real numbers and let ${f \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}}$ be measurable. The following are equivalent.

**(1)** The function $f$ is differentiable almost everywhere on ${(a, b),}$ with ${f' \in L^1((a, b)),}$ and

$$
f(y) - f(x) = \int_x^y f' \, d\lambda \qquad \text{for all } x, y \in [a, b] \text{ with } x < y.
$$

**(2)** ${f \in \mathrm{AC}([a, b]).}$\
In particular, let ${g \in L^1((a, b))}$ and ${F(x) = \int_a^x g \, d\lambda}$ for ${x \in [a, b].}$ Then ${F \in \mathrm{AC}([a, b]).}$

The last claim of Theorem 14 follows from the implication from (1) to (2), since $F$ satisfies (1) by Theorem 9. Indeed, $F$ is continuous, hence measurable, and ${F' = g}$ almost everywhere, so that ${F' \in L^1((a, b))}$ and ${\int_x^y F' \, d\lambda = \int_x^y g \, d\lambda = F(y) - F(x).}$

**Proof (sketch).** Only the implication from (1) to (2) is proved. Fix ${\varepsilon > 0}$ and let ${\delta > 0}$ be given by Lemma 13, applied on ${([a, b], \mathcal{L}([a, b]), \lambda)}$ to ${f' \in L^1.}$ This means that, for every ${E \in \mathcal{L}([a, b]),}$

$$
\lambda(E) < \delta \implies \int_E |f'| \, d\lambda < \varepsilon.
$$

Now we prove that ${f \in \mathrm{AC}([a, b]).}$ Let ${\{ (a_i, b_i) : i = 1, \dots, m \}}$ be pairwise disjoint subintervals of ${[a, b]}$ with total length less than ${\delta.}$ Since the intervals are pairwise disjoint,

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m} (b_i - a_i) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \lambda((a_i, b_i)) = \lambda\Big( \bigcup_{i=1}^{m} (a_i, b_i) \Big) < \delta.
$$

We compute, as in Figure 12,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i=1}^{m} |f(b_i) - f(a_i)| &= \sum_{i=1}^{m} \Big| \int_{a_i}^{b_i} f' \, d\lambda \Big| \\
&\le \sum_{i=1}^{m} \int_{(a_i, b_i)} |f'| \, d\lambda \\
&= \int_{\bigcup_{i=1}^{m} (a_i, b_i)} |f'| \, d\lambda < \varepsilon,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the first equality is (1), and the last inequality is the choice of ${\delta,}$ as the integral is over a set $E$ with ${\lambda(E) < \delta.}$ $\blacksquare$

**Example 15.** Let ${v \colon [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}}$ be the Cantor function of Example 8 of the third post. The function $v$ is continuous on ${[0, 1],}$ but ${v \notin \mathrm{AC}([0, 1])}$ by Theorem 14. Indeed, otherwise (1) of Theorem 14 would give ${v(1) - v(0) = \int_0^1 v' \, d\lambda = 0,}$ while ${v(1) - v(0) = 1}$ by Example 8 of the third post. Still, ${v \in \mathrm{UC}([0, 1])}$ by the [Heine–Cantor theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heine%E2%80%93Cantor_theorem): if ${u \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}}$ is continuous, then ${u \in \mathrm{UC}([a, b]).}$

**Example 16.** There exists ${g \in \mathrm{AC}([0, 1])}$ that is not Lipschitz, for example ${g(x) = \sqrt{x}}$ for ${x \in [0, 1].}$ Indeed, ${(g(x) - g(0))/x = 1/\sqrt{x}}$ is unbounded on ${(0, 1].}$ It satisfies ${g'(x) = 1/(2\sqrt{x})}$ for every ${x \in (0, 1)}$ and

$$
g(y) - g(x) = \int_x^y \frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}} \, dt \qquad \text{for all } x, y \in [0, 1] \text{ with } x < y,
$$

so that ${g \in \mathrm{AC}([0, 1])}$ by Theorem 14, as in Figure 13. In detail, ${g' \in L^1((0, 1))}$ by (2) of Theorem 17 of the fifth post, because ${g' \ge 0}$ and its generalized Riemann integral over $(0, 1)$ is ${g(1) - g(0) = 1.}$ Figure 14 places $g$ and the Cantor function $v$ among the classes of Remark 12.

## Sobolev spaces (introduction)

Sobolev spaces are defined through the identity of Corollary 17, with a function $w$ in place of ${f'.}$

**Definition 18 (Sobolev space ${W^{1,1},}$ weak derivative).** Let ${-\infty \le a < b \le +\infty}$ and ${f \in L^1((a, b)).}$ We say that ${f \in W^{1,1}((a, b)),}$ the *Sobolev space* $W^{1,1}$ on ${(a, b),}$ if there exists ${w \in L^1((a, b))}$ such that

$$
\int_a^b f \varphi' \, d\lambda = -\int_a^b w \varphi \, d\lambda \qquad \text{for every } \varphi \in C_c^\infty(a, b).
$$

The function $w$ is called a *weak derivative* of ${f.}$

**Remark 19.** In the same way one can take ${f \in L^p((a, b))}$ with a weak derivative ${w \in L^p((a, b)),}$ in the sense of the identity of Definition 18. In this case one writes ${f \in W^{1,p}((a, b)).}$

**Theorem 20.** Let ${-\infty < a < b < +\infty.}$ Then ${\mathrm{AC}([a, b]) = W^{1,1}((a, b));}$ more precisely, ${f \in \mathrm{AC}([a, b])}$ if and only if ${f \in W^{1,1}((a, b)),}$ where $f$ is identified with its class in ${L^1((a, b)).}$ In particular, every ${f \in W^{1,1}((a, b))}$ has a continuous representative.

**Proof (complete).** First, let ${f \in \mathrm{AC}([a, b]).}$ By Theorem 14 and Corollary 17, $f'$ exists almost everywhere, ${f' \in L^1((a, b)),}$ and ${\int_a^b f \varphi' \, d\lambda = -\int_a^b f' \varphi \, d\lambda}$ for every ${\varphi \in C_c^\infty(a, b).}$ Hence ${f \in W^{1,1}((a, b)).}$ Indeed, also ${f \in L^1((a, b)),}$ because $f$ is continuous on ${[a, b]}$ by Remark 12, hence bounded, and ${b - a < +\infty.}$

Conversely, let ${f \in W^{1,1}((a, b))}$ and let $w$ be a weak derivative of ${f.}$ Let ${F(x) = \int_a^x w \, d\lambda}$ for ${x \in [a, b].}$ Then ${F \in \mathrm{AC}([a, b])}$ by Theorem 14, and $F'$ exists and ${F' = w}$ almost everywhere by Theorem 9. Let ${\varphi \in C_c^\infty(a, b).}$ Then

$$
\int_a^b F \varphi' \, d\lambda = -\int_a^b F' \varphi \, d\lambda = -\int_a^b w \varphi \, d\lambda = \int_a^b f \varphi' \, d\lambda,
$$

where the first equality is Corollary 17, the second holds because ${F' = w}$ almost everywhere, and the third because ${f \in W^{1,1}((a, b)).}$ Hence

$$
\int_a^b (F - f) \varphi' \, d\lambda = 0 \qquad \text{for every } \varphi \in C_c^\infty(a, b).
$$

By the constancy lemma, (2) of Corollary 16 of the fifth post, there exists ${C \in \mathbb{R}}$ such that ${F - f = C}$ almost everywhere, as in Figure 16. Indeed, ${F - f \in L^1((a, b)),}$ because $F$ is continuous on ${[a, b],}$ hence bounded.

<Representative />

**Figure 16.** An instance of the second part of the proof: the dashed graph of ${F,}$ the graph of ${F - C,}$ at vertical distance $C$ below it, and a representative $f$ of the class. It equals ${F - C}$ except at 3 points, so that ${f = F - C}$ almost everywhere.

Hence ${f = F - C}$ almost everywhere, where ${F \in \mathrm{AC}([a, b])}$ and $C$ is a constant, so $f$ is equal almost everywhere to a function of ${\mathrm{AC}([a, b]).}$ Therefore $f$ has a representative in ${\mathrm{AC}([a, b]).}$ $\blacksquare$
