The simple functions and the measurable functions of the previous post lead to a new definition of the integral on a measure space, the Lebesgue integral. On intervals of $\mathbb{R}$ the new integral is then compared with the [Riemann integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_integral).

## Lebesgue integral

The aim is a new definition of the integral. Its idea is drawn in Figure 1: first the integral of a simple function over a set ${E,}$ then simple functions placed below the graph of a function ${f.}$

<Idea />

**Figure 1.** On the left, a simple function, constant on each of the sets ${D_1,}$ $D_2$ and ${D_3,}$ with the region under its graph hatched, and a set $E$ over which that region is shaded. On the right, a function $f$ and, below its graph, the region under a simple function.

### Nonnegative functions

**Definition 1 (Lebesgue integral of a simple function).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space, let ${s \colon X \to [0, +\infty]}$ be a simple function, written as in Definition 16 of the previous post,

$$
s = \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i \chi_{D_i},
$$

and let ${E \in \mathcal{M}.}$ We define the *Lebesgue integral* of $s$ over $E$ as

$$
\int_E s \, d\mu := \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i \, \mu(E \cap D_i),
$$

where ${+\infty \cdot 0 = 0,}$ as in Definition 5 of the first post.

**Remark 2.** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu),}$ $E$ and $s$ be as in Definition 1. Then $\chi_E$ is simple, ${s \chi_E}$ is simple, and

$$
\int_E s \, d\mu = \int_X s \chi_E \, d\mu.
$$

<Restrict />

**Figure 2.** An instance: on the left, the simple function $s$ of Figure 1 and the region under its graph over ${E;}$ on the right, the simple function ${s \chi_E,}$ equal to $s$ on $E$ and to $0$ outside ${E,}$ and the region under its graph over ${X.}$

**Definition 3 (Lebesgue integral of a nonnegative function).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space, let ${f \colon X \to [0, +\infty]}$ be measurable and let ${E \in \mathcal{M}.}$ We define the Lebesgue integral of $f$ over $E$ as

$$
\int_E f \, d\mu := \sup \Big\{ \int_E s \, d\mu : s \text{ simple}, \ 0 \le s \le f \Big\}.
$$

**Remark 4 (Basic properties).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space, let ${f_1, f_2 \colon X \to [0, +\infty]}$ be measurable, and let ${N, E, F \in \mathcal{M}.}$\
**(1)** If ${\mu(N) = 0,}$ then ${\int_N f_1 \, d\mu = 0.}$\
**(2)** If ${f_1 \le f_2,}$ then ${\int_E f_1 \, d\mu \le \int_E f_2 \, d\mu.}$\
**(3)** If ${E \subset F,}$ then ${\int_E f_1 \, d\mu \le \int_F f_1 \, d\mu.}$\
**(4)** If ${0 \le c \le +\infty,}$ then ${\int_E c f_1 \, d\mu = c \int_E f_1 \, d\mu.}$\
**(5)** ${\int_E (f_1 + f_2) \, d\mu = \int_E f_1 \, d\mu + \int_E f_2 \, d\mu.}$\
**(6)** If ${E \cap F = \emptyset,}$ then ${\int_E f_1 \, d\mu + \int_F f_1 \, d\mu = \int_{E \cup F} f_1 \, d\mu.}$\
Properties (2) and (3) are called *monotonicity*, and properties (4) and (5) *linearity*.

**Proposition 5 (Measure induced by simple functions).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space, let ${s \colon X \to [0, +\infty]}$ be simple, and let ${\nu \colon \mathcal{M} \to [0, +\infty]}$ be defined by

$$
\nu(E) := \int_E s \, d\mu.
$$

Then $\nu$ is a measure.

**Proof (sketch).** Only the $\sigma$-additivity of $\nu$ is proved. Let ${(E_m) \subset \mathcal{M}}$ be pairwise disjoint, let ${E = \bigcup_m E_m,}$ and write $s$ as in Definition 1, so that ${D_i \cap E = \bigcup_m (D_i \cap E_m)}$ for every ${i,}$ as in Figure 3. The fourth equality below is the $\sigma$-additivity of ${\mu{:}}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nu(E) &= \int_E s \, d\mu = \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i \, \mu(D_i \cap E) \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i \, \mu\Big( \bigcup_m (D_i \cap E_m) \Big) \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \mu(D_i \cap E_m) \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i \lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{m=0}^{N} \mu(D_i \cap E_m) \\
&= \lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{m=0}^{N} \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i \, \mu(D_i \cap E_m) \\
&= \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i \, \mu(D_i \cap E_m) = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \int_{E_m} s \, d\mu,
\end{aligned}
$$

and ${\int_{E_m} s \, d\mu = \nu(E_m)}$ for every ${m.}$ $\blacksquare$

**Remark 6.** Let ${X = \mathbb{N},}$ ${\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})}$ and ${\mu = \mu_\#,}$ the counting measure of Example 3 of the second post, on the subsets of ${\mathbb{N}.}$ In this case a function ${f \colon \mathbb{N} \to [0, +\infty]}$ is a sequence ${(f_m),}$ with ${f_m = f(m) \ge 0,}$ as in Figure 4, and

$$
\int_{\mathbb{N}} f \, d\mu_\# = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} f_m.
$$

**Figure 4.** The drawing of Remark 6: the values ${f_0,}$ ${f_1,}$ $f_2$ and $f_3$ of a sequence, as bars over the points ${0,}$ ${1,}$ $2$ and $3$ of ${\mathbb{N}.}$

**Remark 7.** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a complete measure space and let ${f, g \colon X \to [0, +\infty]}$ be measurable. If ${f = g}$ almost everywhere, then

$$
\int_X f \, d\mu = \int_X g \, d\mu.
$$

**Proof (complete).** Let ${N = \{ x \in X : f(x) \neq g(x) \},}$ so that ${\mu(N) = 0}$ by Definition 8 of the previous post. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_X f \, d\mu &= \int_{X \setminus N} f \, d\mu + \int_N f \, d\mu \\
&= \int_{X \setminus N} g \, d\mu \\
&= \int_{X \setminus N} g \, d\mu + \int_N g \, d\mu = \int_X g \, d\mu.
\end{aligned}
$$

Indeed, the first and the last equalities are (6) of Remark 4, the integrals over $N$ are $0$ by (1) of Remark 4, and ${f = g}$ on ${X \setminus N.}$ $\blacksquare$

**Example 8.** On $\mathbb{R}$ with the Lebesgue measure ${\lambda,}$ the function $\chi_{\mathbb{Q}}$ satisfies ${\chi_{\mathbb{Q}} = 0}$ almost everywhere, as in Example 9 (ii) of the previous post. Hence ${\int_{\mathbb{R}} \chi_{\mathbb{Q}} \, d\lambda = 0}$ by Remark 7. The Riemann integral of this function does not exist.

**Proposition 9 (Chebyshev inequality, or Markov inequality).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space, let ${f \colon X \to [0, +\infty]}$ be measurable and let ${c \in (0, +\infty).}$ Then

$$
c \, \mu(\{ f > c \}) \le \int_{\{ f > c \}} f \, d\mu \le \int_X f \, d\mu,
$$

where ${\{ f > c \} = \{ x \in X : f(x) > c \}.}$

**Proof (complete).** By monotonicity with respect to the domain of integration, (3) of Remark 4, and by monotonicity with respect to the function, (2) of Remark 4,

$$
\int_X f \, d\mu \ge \int_{\{ f > c \}} f \, d\mu \ge \int_{\{ f > c \}} c \, d\mu = c \, \mu(\{ f > c \}). \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

From now on, the measure space ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ is assumed complete.

**Corollary 10.** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a complete measure space, let ${f \colon X \to [0, +\infty]}$ be measurable and let ${E \in \mathcal{M}.}$\
**(1)** ${\int_E f \, d\mu = 0}$ if and only if ${f = 0}$ almost everywhere on ${E.}$ This is the *vanishing lemma for nonnegative functions*.\
**(2)** If ${\int_X f \, d\mu < +\infty,}$ then ${\mu(\{ f = +\infty \}) = 0.}$

**Proof (sketch).** Only (1) is proved. If ${f = 0}$ almost everywhere on ${E,}$ then ${\int_E f \, d\mu = 0}$ by Remark 7. Conversely, suppose that ${\int_E f \, d\mu = 0,}$ and for every ${m \in \mathbb{N}}$ with ${m \ge 1}$ let

$$
E_m = \Big\{ x \in E : f(x) > \frac{1}{m} \Big\}.
$$

Then

$$
\bigcup_m E_m = \{ x \in E : f(x) > 0 \},
$$

and the goal is to prove that this set has measure zero, as in Figure 6. By Proposition 9 with ${c = 1/m,}$ applied on ${E,}$

$$
\mu(E_m) \le m \int_E f \, d\mu = 0,
$$

so ${\mu(E_m) = 0}$ for every ${m.}$ Hence, by the $\sigma$-subadditivity of Proposition 7 of the second post,

$$
\mu\Big( \bigcup_m E_m \Big) \le \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \mu(E_m) = 0. \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

### Real valued functions

Definition 3 applies to measurable functions with values in ${[0, +\infty].}$ For a measurable ${f \colon X \to \mathbb{R},}$ the positive part $f^+$ and the negative part $f^-$ of Example 6 of the previous post are such functions, and so is ${|f|.}$

**Definition 11 (Integrable function, Lebesgue integral).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space and let ${f \colon X \to \mathbb{R}}$ be measurable.\
**(i)** The function $f$ is said to be *integrable* if ${\int_X |f| \, d\mu < +\infty.}$

**(ii)** If $f$ is integrable, we define the Lebesgue integral of $f$ as the real number

$$
\int_X f \, d\mu := \int_X f^+ \, d\mu - \int_X f^- \, d\mu.
$$

**(iii)** If ${E \in \mathcal{M}}$ and $f$ is integrable, we define the real number

$$
\int_E f \, d\mu := \int_X f \chi_E \, d\mu.
$$

<Parts />

**Figure 7.** The idea of Definition 11: a function ${f,}$ its positive part ${f^+,}$ and its negative part ${f^-,}$ dashed, which is the part of $f$ below the axis reflected above it.

**Notation.** The integrable functions form the set

$$
\mathcal{L}^1(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu) := \{ f \colon X \to \mathbb{R} : f \text{ integrable} \},
$$

also written ${\mathcal{L}^1(X)}$ or ${\mathcal{L}^1.}$ The calligraphic $\mathcal{L}^1$ is different from ${L^1{:}}$ ${\mathcal{L}^1 \neq L^1.}$

**Remark 12.** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space and let ${f \colon X \to \mathbb{R}}$ be measurable.\
**(1)** ${f \in \mathcal{L}^1}$ if and only if ${|f| \in \mathcal{L}^1,}$ if and only if $f^+$ and $f^-$ belong to ${\mathcal{L}^1.}$\
**(2)** If ${f \in \mathcal{L}^1,}$ then ${\big| \int_X f \, d\mu \big| \le \int_X |f| \, d\mu.}$

**Proof (sketch).** Only (2) is proved:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Big| \int_X f \, d\mu \Big| &= \Big| \int_X f^+ \, d\mu - \int_X f^- \, d\mu \Big| \\
&\le \Big| \int_X f^+ \, d\mu \Big| + \Big| \int_X f^- \, d\mu \Big| \\
&= \int_X f^+ \, d\mu + \int_X f^- \, d\mu = \int_X (f^+ + f^-) \, d\mu = \int_X |f| \, d\mu,
\end{aligned}
$$

since ${f^+ + f^- = |f|.}$ $\blacksquare$

**Remark 13.** The set ${\mathcal{L}^1(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ is a vector space, and the map

$$
\int_X \cdot \, d\mu \colon \mathcal{L}^1 \to \mathbb{R}, \qquad f \mapsto \int_X f \, d\mu,
$$

is linear, that is,

$$
\int_X (\alpha f + \beta g) \, d\mu = \alpha \int_X f \, d\mu + \beta \int_X g \, d\mu
$$

for all ${\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}}$ and ${f, g \in \mathcal{L}^1.}$

<Linear />

**Figure 8.** An instance: the map ${f \mapsto \int_X f \, d\mu}$ sends ${f,}$ $g$ and ${\alpha f + \beta g}$ in $\mathcal{L}^1$ to ${\int_X f \, d\mu,}$ $\int_X g \, d\mu$ and ${\alpha \int_X f \, d\mu + \beta \int_X g \, d\mu}$ in ${\mathbb{R}.}$

**Proposition 14 (Vanishing lemma for real valued functions).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a complete measure space and let ${f, g \in \mathcal{L}^1(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu).}$ The following are equivalent:\
**(1)** ${f = g}$ almost everywhere;\
**(2)** ${\int_X |f - g| \, d\mu = 0;}$\
**(3)** ${\int_E f \, d\mu = \int_E g \, d\mu}$ for every ${E \in \mathcal{M}.}$\
In particular, if ${\int_E f \, d\mu = 0}$ for every ${E \in \mathcal{M},}$ that is, if ${\int_X f \chi_E \, d\mu = 0}$ for every ${E \in \mathcal{M},}$ then ${f = 0}$ almost everywhere.

**Proof (complete).** We prove that (1) implies (2), that (2) implies (3) and that (3) implies (1). If (1) holds, then (2) holds. Indeed, ${|f - g| = 0}$ almost everywhere, so ${\int_X |f - g| \, d\mu = 0}$ by Remark 7.

To prove that (2) implies ${(3),}$ let ${E \in \mathcal{M}.}$ Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Big| \int_E f \, d\mu - \int_E g \, d\mu \Big| &= \Big| \int_E (f - g) \, d\mu \Big| \\
&\le \int_E |f - g| \, d\mu \le \int_X |f - g| \, d\mu = 0,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the first inequality is (2) of Remark 12, the second is monotonicity and the last equality is the hypothesis (2).

To prove that (3) implies ${(1),}$ let ${E = \{ x \in X : f(x) \ge g(x) \}.}$ We prove that ${f = g}$ almost everywhere on ${E;}$ in the same way one gets ${f = g}$ almost everywhere on ${\{ f \le g \},}$ as in Figure 9. By ${(3),}$

$$
0 = \int_E f \, d\mu - \int_E g \, d\mu = \int_E (f - g) \, d\mu,
$$

where ${f - g \ge 0}$ on ${E.}$ Hence ${f - g = 0}$ almost everywhere on $E$ by the vanishing lemma for nonnegative functions, (1) of Corollary 10. $\blacksquare$

**Notation.** The set of the *continuous functions with compact support*, the continuous functions from $\mathbb{R}^N$ to $\mathbb{R}$ whose [support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Support_(mathematics)) is compact, is denoted ${C_c(\mathbb{R}^N).}$

**Theorem 15 (Vanishing for $\lambda$ with respect to other classes of functions).** Let ${f \colon \mathbb{R}^N \to \mathbb{R}}$ with ${f \in \mathcal{L}^1(\mathbb{R}^N, \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^N), \lambda).}$ If

$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^N} f g \, d\lambda = 0 \qquad \text{for every } g \in C_c(\mathbb{R}^N),
$$

then ${f = 0}$ almost everywhere.

**Notation.** For an interval of ${\mathbb{R},}$ such as $(a, b)$ or ${[a, b],}$ the family of the sets of $\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R})$ contained in it is written ${\mathcal{L}((a, b))}$ or ${\mathcal{L}([a, b]).}$ The set $C_c((a, b))$ is defined as $C_c(\mathbb{R}^N)$ with $(a, b)$ in place of ${\mathbb{R}^N,}$ the set $C^\infty((a, b))$ is the set of the infinitely differentiable functions on ${(a, b),}$ and

$$
C_c^\infty(a, b) := C_c((a, b)) \cap C^\infty((a, b)).
$$

**Corollary 16 (Vanishing and constancy on intervals).** Let ${f \in \mathcal{L}^1((a, b), \mathcal{L}((a, b)), \lambda).}$\
**(1)** If ${\int_{(a, b)} f g \, d\lambda = 0}$ for every ${g \in C_c^\infty(a, b),}$ then ${f = 0}$ almost everywhere.\
**(2)** If ${\int_{(a, b)} f g' \, d\lambda = 0}$ for every ${g \in C_c^\infty(a, b),}$ then there exists ${c \in \mathbb{R}}$ such that ${f = c}$ almost everywhere.\
**(3)** If ${\int_{(a, b)} f g \, d\lambda = 0}$ for every ${g \in C_c^\infty(a, b)}$ such that ${\int_{(a, b)} g \, d\lambda = 0,}$ then there exists ${c \in \mathbb{R}}$ such that ${f = c}$ almost everywhere.

## Lebesgue versus Riemann integral

On intervals of $\mathbb{R}$ with the Lebesgue measure ${\lambda,}$ the integral of Definition 11 is compared with the Riemann integral.

**Notation.** In this part, ${\int_{(a, b)} f \, d\lambda}$ denotes the Lebesgue integral and ${\int_a^b f \, dx}$ the Riemann integral.

**Theorem 17 (Comparison of Lebesgue and Riemann integrals).** **(1)** Let ${a, b \in \mathbb{R}}$ with ${a < b,}$ and let ${f \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}}$ be Riemann integrable. Then ${f \in \mathcal{L}^1([a, b], \mathcal{L}([a, b]), \lambda)}$ and

$$
\int_{[a, b]} f \, d\lambda = \int_a^b f \, dx.
$$

**(2)** Let ${-\infty \le a < b \le +\infty,}$ and let ${f \colon (a, b) \to \mathbb{R}}$ be Riemann integrable on every closed bounded interval contained in ${(a, b),}$ with $|f|$ [Riemann integrable in the generalized sense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Improper_integral). Then ${f \in \mathcal{L}^1((a, b), \mathcal{L}((a, b)), \lambda)}$ and

$$
\int_{(a, b)} f \, d\lambda = \int_a^b f \, dx,
$$

where the right-hand side is the Riemann integral in the generalized sense.

### The function $(\sin x)/x$

**Example 18.** Let ${f \colon [\pi, +\infty) \to \mathbb{R}}$ be given by ${f(x) = (\sin x)/x.}$\
**(i)** The function $f$ is Riemann integrable in the generalized sense. Integration by parts gives, for every ${R > \pi,}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_\pi^R \frac{\sin x}{x} \, dx &= -\frac{\cos x}{x} \Big|_\pi^R - \int_\pi^R \frac{-\cos x}{-x^2} \, dx \\
&= -\frac{\cos R}{R} - \frac{1}{\pi} - \int_\pi^R \frac{\cos x}{x^2} \, dx,
\end{aligned}
$$

where ${(\cos R)/R \to 0}$ as ${R \to +\infty}$ and ${|(\cos x)/x^2| \le 1/x^2.}$ Hence the limit

$$
\lim_{R \to +\infty} \int_\pi^R \frac{\sin x}{x} \, dx
$$

exists and is finite.

**(ii)** The function $f$ is not Lebesgue integrable on ${[\pi, +\infty).}$ In fact,

$$
\int_{[\pi, +\infty)} \Big| \frac{\sin x}{x} \Big| \, d\lambda = +\infty.
$$

Let ${N \in \mathbb{N}}$ with ${N \ge 1.}$ For ${1 \le m \le N,}$ on ${[m\pi, (m+1)\pi)}$ we have ${x \le (m+1)\pi;}$ moreover, the function $|\sin x|$ is ${\pi}$-periodic and ${\int_{[0, \pi]} |\sin x| \, d\lambda = 2.}$ Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{[\pi, +\infty)} \Big| \frac{\sin x}{x} \Big| \, d\lambda &\ge \int_{[\pi, (N+1)\pi]} \Big| \frac{\sin x}{x} \Big| \, d\lambda \\
&= \sum_{m=1}^{N} \int_{[m\pi, (m+1)\pi)} \frac{|\sin x|}{x} \, d\lambda \\
&\ge \sum_{m=1}^{N} \frac{1}{(m+1)\pi} \int_{[m\pi, (m+1)\pi)} |\sin x| \, d\lambda \\
&= \sum_{m=1}^{N} \frac{1}{(m+1)\pi} \int_{[0, \pi]} |\sin x| \, d\lambda = \sum_{m=1}^{N} \frac{2}{\pi(m+1)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This holds for every ${N \ge 1,}$ hence

$$
\int_{[\pi, +\infty)} \Big| \frac{\sin x}{x} \Big| \, d\lambda \ge \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \frac{2}{\pi(m+1)} = +\infty,
$$

since the last series behaves like the [harmonic series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_series_(mathematics)).

From now on, ${\int_a^b f}$ and ${\int_a^b f \, d\lambda}$ denote Lebesgue integrals over intervals, and every integral is understood in the sense of Lebesgue.
