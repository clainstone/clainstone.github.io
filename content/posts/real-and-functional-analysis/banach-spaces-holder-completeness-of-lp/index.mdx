Functional analysis studies infinite-dimensional vector spaces (Brezis).

## Normed and Banach spaces

**Definition 1 (Norm).** Let $X$ be a vector space. A *norm* on $X$ is a function ${\|\cdot\| \colon X \to [0, +\infty)}$ such that, for all ${x, y \in X}$ and ${\alpha \in \mathbb{R}{:}}$\
**(i)** ${\|x\| = 0}$ if and only if ${x = 0;}$\
**(ii)** ${\|\alpha x\| = |\alpha| \, \|x\|;}$\
**(iii)** ${\|x + y\| \le \|x\| + \|y\|,}$ the *triangular inequality*, drawn in Figure 1.\
A norm $\|\cdot\|$ induces on $X$ the distance ${d \colon X \times X \to [0, +\infty)}$ given by ${d(x, y) = \|x - y\|,}$ and $(X, d)$ is a metric space.

**Figure 1.** The drawing of Definition 1 (iii), an instance in the plane: two vectors $x$ and ${y,}$ their sum ${x + y}$ and the vector $y$ drawn again from the end of ${x.}$ Dashed, the segment from the end of $y$ to the end of ${x + y.}$

**Remark 2 (Normed space).** If $\|\cdot\|$ is a norm on a vector space ${X,}$ the couple $(X, \|\cdot\|)$ is called a *normed space*. Balls, open, closed and compact sets in $X$ and continuous functions ${f \colon X \to \mathbb{R}}$ are then well defined: they are those of the metric space $(X, d)$ of Definition 1, as in Definitions 12, 13, 17 and 19 of the first post.

**Remark 3.** Let $(X, \|\cdot\|)$ be a normed space. Then the norm ${\|\cdot\| \colon X \to [0, +\infty)}$ is continuous.

**Proof (complete).** The norm satisfies the *reverse triangle inequality* ${\big| \|x\| - \|y\| \big| \le \|x - y\|}$ for all ${x, y \in X.}$ Let ${(x_n) \subset X}$ and ${x_0 \in X}$ be such that ${x_n \to x_0}$ in ${X.}$ This means ${d(x_n, x_0) \to 0,}$ that is, ${\|x_n - x_0\| \to 0.}$ We want to prove that ${\|x_n\| \to \|x_0\|,}$ and the reverse triangle inequality gives it, as in Figure 2: ${\big| \|x_n\| - \|x_0\| \big| \le \|x_n - x_0\| \to 0.}$ $\blacksquare$

**Definition 4 (Cauchy sequence, Banach space).** Let $(X, \|\cdot\|)$ be a normed space.\
**(i)** A sequence $(x_n)$ of points of $X$ is *Cauchy* if for every ${\varepsilon > 0}$ there exists $N$ such that ${\|x_n - x_m\| < \varepsilon}$ for all ${n, m \ge N,}$ as in Figure 3. Here ${\|x_n - x_m\| = d(x_n, x_m).}$\
**(ii)** If every Cauchy sequence converges in ${X,}$ we say that $(X, \|\cdot\|)$ is a *Banach space*.

### Norms on Euclidean space and on continuous functions

**Example 5.** **(i)** Let $N$ be a positive integer. The *Euclidean space* ${(\mathbb{R}^N, |\cdot|)}$ is a Banach space, where

$$
|(v_1, \dots, v_N)| = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N} (v_i)^2}.
$$

**(ii)** For ${p \in [1, +\infty),}$ consider ${(\mathbb{R}^N, \|\cdot\|_p),}$ where

$$
\|(v_1, \dots, v_N)\|_p = \Big( \sum_{i=1}^{N} |v_i|^p \Big)^{\frac{1}{p}}, \qquad \text{and also} \qquad \|(v_1, \dots, v_N)\|_\infty = \max_{i = 1, \dots, N} |v_i|.
$$

Notice that ${|\cdot| = \|\cdot\|_2.}$ The spaces ${(\mathbb{R}^N, \|\cdot\|_p)}$ are Banach spaces.

**(iii)** Let ${-\infty < a < b < +\infty,}$ and let $C([a, b])$ be the set of the continuous functions on ${[a, b],}$ with

$$
\|u\|_\infty = \operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{[a, b]} |u| = \max_{[a, b]} |u| \qquad \text{for } u \in C([a, b]).
$$

The essential supremum is that of Definition 19 of the sixth post, with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and it is a maximum because $u$ is continuous. The space ${(C([a, b]), \|\cdot\|_\infty)}$ is a Banach space.

In Example 5 with ${N = 2,}$ the balls $B_1(0)$ of Definition 19 of the first post, of radius 1 and centre ${0,}$ are drawn in Figure 4 for the norms ${|\cdot|,}$ $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ and ${\|\cdot\|_1,}$ and in Figure 5 for ${\|\cdot\|_p.}$ If ${v_1, v_2 > 0,}$ then ${\|(v_1, v_2)\|_1 = v_1 + v_2.}$ Hence the points of this quadrant with ${\|(v_1, v_2)\|_1 \le 1}$ are those with ${v_1 + v_2 \le 1.}$

### Convergent series

**Definition 6 (Convergent series).** Let $(X, \|\cdot\|)$ be a normed space and let $(x_n)$ be a sequence of points of ${X.}$ For every $N$ let

$$
S_N = \sum_{n=0}^{N} x_n.
$$

If there exists ${S \in X}$ such that ${S_N \to S}$ in ${X,}$ that is, ${\|S_N - S\| \to 0,}$ we say that the series $\sum_n x_n$ *converges* to $S$ in ${X,}$ as in Figure 7.

**Proposition 7 (Criterion to distinguish Banach spaces).** Let $(X, \|\cdot\|)$ be a normed space. Then $(X, \|\cdot\|)$ is a Banach space if and only if, for every sequence $(x_n)$ of points of $X$ such that ${\sum_n \|x_n\| < +\infty,}$ the series $\sum_n x_n$ converges in ${X.}$

Below, the lengths $\|x_n\|$ of the pieces are laid end to end, and their sum is finite, as in Proposition 7.

## The $L^p$ spaces (part 2)

The spaces $L^p(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)$ of Definitions 14 and 19 of the sixth post are now studied with the notions of Definitions 1 and 4.

### The spaces $\ell^p$

**Example 8.** Let ${p \in [1, +\infty].}$\
**(i)** Examples of the spaces $L^p(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)$ are ${L^p(\mathbb{R}^N),}$ $L^p((a, b))$ and so on, with the Lebesgue measure.

**(ii)** Let ${\ell^p := L^p(\mathbb{N}, \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}), \mu_\#),}$ where $\mu_\#$ is the counting measure of Example 3 of the second post, on the subsets of ${\mathbb{N}.}$ The elements ${x \in \ell^p}$ are sequences, written ${x = (x^{(k)}),}$ where ${x^{(k)} \in \mathbb{R}}$ is the $k$-th value of the sequence ${x.}$ Indeed, the only set of counting measure zero is the empty set, so each class of $\ell^p$ contains exactly one function from $\mathbb{N}$ to ${\mathbb{R}.}$ For ${p < +\infty}$ the $\ell^p$ norm of ${x \in \ell^p}$ is

$$
\|x\|_p = \Big( \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} |x^{(k)}|^p \Big)^{\frac{1}{p}}.
$$

In detail, the sum is the integral of $|x|^p$ with respect to $\mu_\#$ by Remark 6 of the fifth post. A sequence in $\ell^p$ is written ${(x_n) \subset \ell^p,}$ with ${x_n \in \ell^p}$ for every ${n,}$ and the $k$-th value of $x_n$ is ${x_n^{(k)} \in \mathbb{R},}$ as in Figure 8.

### Conjugate exponents and the Hölder inequality

**Definition 9 (Conjugate exponent).** Let ${p \in [1, +\infty].}$ The *conjugate* of $p$ is the exponent $p'$ such that:\
**(i)** if ${p \in (1, +\infty),}$ then ${p' \in (1, +\infty)}$ and ${1/p + 1/p' = 1,}$ as in Figure 9;\
**(ii)** if ${p = 1,}$ then ${p' = +\infty;}$\
**(iii)** if ${p = +\infty,}$ then ${p' = 1.}$

**Remark 10.** **(i)** The exponent $p'$ is the conjugate of $p$ if and only if $p$ is the conjugate of ${p'.}$\
**(ii)** If ${p = 2,}$ then ${p' = 2.}$

<Conjugates client:visible />

**Figure 9.** The pairs $(p, p')$ of Definition 9 (i) for ${p \in [1.2, 6],}$ with the point $(p, p')$ for the $p$ of the slider and the point ${(p', p),}$ which is on the curve by Remark 10 (i). The dashed diagonal meets the curve at ${(2, 2),}$ as in Remark 10 (ii).

**Lemma 11 (Young inequality).** Let ${1 < p < +\infty,}$ let $p'$ be its conjugate, and let ${a, b \in \mathbb{R}}$ with ${a, b > 0.}$ Then

$$
ab \le \frac{a^p}{p} + \frac{b^{p'}}{p'}.
$$

**Proof (complete).** The logarithm is *concave*, as in Figure 10, that is, for all ${\alpha, \beta > 0}$ and ${t \in (0, 1),}$

$$
\log\big( t\alpha + (1 - t)\beta \big) \ge t \log \alpha + (1 - t) \log \beta.
$$

Since ${1/p + 1/p' = 1,}$ concavity applies with ${t = 1/p}$ and ${1 - t = 1/p'.}$ With ${\alpha = a^p}$ and ${\beta = b^{p'}}$ it gives

$$
\log\Big( \frac{a^p}{p} + \frac{b^{p'}}{p'} \Big) \ge \frac{1}{p} \log(a^p) + \frac{1}{p'} \log(b^{p'}) = \log a + \log b = \log(ab).
$$

Applying the exponential function to both sides gives ${e^{\log(a^p/p + b^{p'}/p')} \ge e^{\log(ab)},}$ which is the claim. $\blacksquare$

Above the abscissa ${t\alpha + (1 - t)\beta}$ with ${t = 1/p,}$ the chord is at height $\log(ab)$ and the graph at height ${\log(a^p/p + b^{p'}/p').}$

**Proposition 12 (Hölder inequality).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space, let ${p \in [1, +\infty]}$ with conjugate ${p',}$ and let ${u, v \colon X \to \mathbb{R}}$ be measurable. Then

$$
\int_X |uv| \, d\mu \le \|u\|_p \, \|v\|_{p'},
$$

where $\|u\|_p$ and $\|v\|_{p'}$ are given by the formulas of Definitions 17 and 19 of the sixth post and may equal ${+\infty,}$ and the product follows Definition 5 of the first post.

**Proof (complete).** Without loss of generality ${\|u\|_p > 0}$ and ${\|v\|_{p'} > 0.}$ Otherwise one of the two functions is $0$ almost everywhere, so that ${|uv| = 0}$ almost everywhere, and the inequality reads ${0 \le 0.}$ In detail, ${\|u\|_p = 0}$ gives ${u = 0}$ almost everywhere by (1) of Corollary 10 of the fifth post, or by Remark 20 of the sixth post if ${p = +\infty,}$ and then the right-hand side is $0$ by Definition 5 of the first post.

Without loss of generality ${\|u\|_p < +\infty}$ and ${\|v\|_{p'} < +\infty.}$ Otherwise the right-hand side is ${+\infty,}$ and the inequality is true.

Without loss of generality ${\|u\|_p = \|v\|_{p'} = 1.}$ To reduce to this case, for $u$ and $v$ as above let

$$
\tilde{u} = \frac{u}{\|u\|_p}, \qquad \tilde{v} = \frac{v}{\|v\|_{p'}}.
$$

Then ${\|\tilde{u}\|_p = \|\tilde{v}\|_{p'} = 1,}$ and the inequality for functions with norms equal to $1$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_X |\tilde{u} \tilde{v}| \, d\mu &\le \|\tilde{u}\|_p \, \|\tilde{v}\|_{p'} = 1, \\
\int_X |\tilde{u} \tilde{v}| \, d\mu &= \frac{1}{\|u\|_p \, \|v\|_{p'}} \int_X |uv| \, d\mu.
\end{aligned}
$$

Indeed, multiplying by ${\|u\|_p \, \|v\|_{p'}}$ gives the inequality for $u$ and ${v.}$ From now on ${\|u\|_p = \|v\|_{p'} = 1.}$ If ${1 < p < +\infty,}$ then, as in Figure 11,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_X |uv| \, d\mu &\le \int_X \Big( \frac{|u|^p}{p} + \frac{|v|^{p'}}{p'} \Big) \, d\mu = \frac{1}{p} \|u\|_p^p + \frac{1}{p'} \|v\|_{p'}^{p'} \\
&= \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{p'} = 1 = \|u\|_p \, \|v\|_{p'},
\end{aligned}
$$

where the inequality is the Young inequality, Lemma 11, at every point of ${X.}$ Indeed, at a point $x$ where ${u(x) = 0}$ or ${v(x) = 0}$ the pointwise inequality holds because its left-hand side is ${0.}$ If ${p = 1,}$ then ${p' = +\infty}$ and

$$
\int_X |uv| \, d\mu = \int_X |u| \, |v| \, d\mu \le \|v\|_\infty \int_X |u| \, d\mu = \|v\|_\infty \, \|u\|_1,
$$

since ${|v| \le \|v\|_\infty}$ almost everywhere by Remark 20 of the sixth post. Indeed, for ${p = +\infty}$ we have ${p' = 1,}$ and the case ${p = 1}$ applies with $u$ and $v$ exchanged. $\blacksquare$

### Inclusions and interpolation

**Corollary 13.** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space such that ${\mu(X) < +\infty,}$ and let ${1 \le p \le q \le +\infty.}$ Then ${L^q \subset L^p,}$ as in Figure 12.

**Proof (complete).** Let ${f \in L^q.}$ We apply the Hölder inequality, Proposition 12, to ${u = |f|^p}$ and ${v = 1,}$ with the exponent ${q/p \ge 1}$ and its conjugate ${r.}$ Here ${1/r = 1 - 1/(q/p) = 1 - p/q.}$ This gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_X |f|^p \, d\mu = \int_X |f|^p \cdot 1 \, d\mu &\le \Big( \int_X |f|^{p \frac{q}{p}} \, d\mu \Big)^{\frac{p}{q}} \Big( \int_X 1^r \, d\mu \Big)^{\frac{1}{r}} \\
&= \|f\|_q^p \, \mu(X)^{\frac{1}{r}} < +\infty,
\end{aligned}
$$

since ${\|f\|_q < +\infty}$ and ${\mu(X) < +\infty.}$ In detail, for ${p = q}$ there is nothing to prove, and for ${q = +\infty}$ Proposition 12 gives in the same way ${\int_X |f|^p \, d\mu \le \|f\|_\infty^p \, \mu(X).}$ $\blacksquare$

**Remark 14.** For all ${1 \le p \le q \le +\infty,}$ ${\ell^1 \subset \ell^p \subset \ell^q \subset \ell^\infty,}$ as in Figure 12.

**Corollary 15 (Interpolation).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space, let ${1 \le p \le r \le q \le +\infty,}$ and let ${u \colon X \to \mathbb{R}}$ be measurable. Then, as in Figure 13,

$$
\|u\|_r \le \|u\|_p^{\alpha} \, \|u\|_q^{1 - \alpha} \qquad \text{with} \qquad \frac{1}{r} = \frac{\alpha}{p} + \frac{1 - \alpha}{q},
$$

where ${\alpha \in [0, 1],}$ ${1/(+\infty) := 0}$ and the norms are as in Proposition 12. In particular, ${L^p \cap L^q \subset L^r.}$

### The Minkowski inequality and the Riesz–Fischer theorem

**Proposition 16 (Minkowski inequality).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space, let ${1 \le p \le +\infty}$ and let ${u, v \in L^p.}$ Then

$$
\|u + v\|_p \le \|u\|_p + \|v\|_p.
$$

In particular, ${(L^p(X), \|\cdot\|_p)}$ is a normed space.

**Proof (sketch).** The case ${p = +\infty}$ is not proved. If ${p = 1,}$ then ${\int_X |u + v| \, d\mu \le \int_X (|u| + |v|) \, d\mu = \|u\|_1 + \|v\|_1.}$ Let ${1 < p < +\infty.}$ Then, as in Figure 14,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|u + v\|_p^p &= \int_X |u + v|^p \, d\mu = \int_X |u + v| \, |u + v|^{p-1} \, d\mu \\
&\le \int_X |u| \, |u + v|^{p-1} \, d\mu + \int_X |v| \, |u + v|^{p-1} \, d\mu,
\end{aligned}
$$

since ${|u + v| \le |u| + |v|.}$ The conjugate of $p$ satisfies ${1/p' = 1 - 1/p = (p - 1)/p,}$ that is, ${p' = p/(p - 1),}$ and the Hölder inequality, Proposition 12, applied to each of the last two integrals gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\int_X |u| \, |u + v|^{p-1} \, d\mu + \int_X |v| \, |u + v|^{p-1} \, d\mu \\
&\quad \le \|u\|_p \Big( \int_X \big( |u + v|^{p-1} \big)^{p'} \, d\mu \Big)^{\frac{1}{p'}} + \|v\|_p \Big( \int_X \big( |u + v|^{p-1} \big)^{p'} \, d\mu \Big)^{\frac{1}{p'}} \\
&\quad = \big( \|u\|_p + \|v\|_p \big) \Big( \int_X |u + v|^p \, d\mu \Big)^{\frac{p-1}{p}} \\
&\quad = \big( \|u\|_p + \|v\|_p \big) \, \|u + v\|_p^{p-1},
\end{aligned}
$$

where ${(p - 1) p' = p.}$ All in all,

$$
\|u + v\|_p^p \le \big( \|u\|_p + \|v\|_p \big) \, \|u + v\|_p^{p-1},
$$

which is equivalent to ${\|u + v\|_p \le \|u\|_p + \|v\|_p.}$ Indeed, ${\|u + v\|_p < +\infty}$ because ${u + v \in L^p}$ by Remark 18 (i) of the sixth post, and for ${\|u + v\|_p > 0}$ one divides by ${\|u + v\|_p^{p-1}.}$ $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 17 (Riesz–Fischer).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space and let ${1 \le p \le +\infty.}$ Then $L^p(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)$ is a Banach space.

**Proof (sketch).** Only the case ${1 \le p < +\infty}$ is treated, with the criterion of Proposition 7. Let ${(f_n) \subset L^p}$ be such that ${\sum_n \|f_n\|_p < +\infty,}$ and let

$$
S_N = \sum_{n=0}^{N} f_n.
$$

The goal is to show that $(S_N)$ converges in ${L^p.}$ Define

$$
g_N(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{N} |f_n(x)|,
$$

so that ${g_N \ge 0}$ and ${g_N \in L^p.}$ Since ${g_{N+1} \ge g_N}$ almost everywhere, we can define ${g(x) = \lim_{N \to \infty} g_N(x)}$ almost everywhere. For every ${N,}$ by the Minkowski inequality, Proposition 16,

$$
\|g_N\|_p = \Big\| \sum_{n=0}^{N} |f_n| \Big\|_p \le \sum_{n=0}^{N} \big\| |f_n| \big\|_p = \sum_{n=0}^{N} \|f_n\|_p \le \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \|f_n\|_p < +\infty.
$$

By the monotone convergence theorem, Theorem 4 of the sixth post, and by this bound,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_X |g|^p \, d\mu &= \lim_{N \to +\infty} \int_X |g_N|^p \, d\mu = \lim_{N \to +\infty} \|g_N\|_p^p \\
&\le \lim_{N \to +\infty} \Big( \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \|f_n\|_p \Big)^p < +\infty,
\end{aligned}
$$

hence ${g \in L^p.}$ Indeed, the functions $|g_N|^p$ are nonnegative and nondecreasing in $N$ almost everywhere, with limit $|g|^p$ almost everywhere. Now, at almost every ${x \in X,}$ as in Figure 15,

$$
|S_N(x)| = \Big| \sum_{n=0}^{N} f_n(x) \Big| \le \sum_{n=0}^{N} |f_n(x)| = g_N(x) \le g(x),
$$

with ${g \in L^p.}$ Moreover, at almost every ${x,}$

$$
g(x) = \lim_{N \to \infty} g_N(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} |f_n(x)| < +\infty,
$$

because ${g \in L^p,}$ so that the series of the $f_n(x)$ is an absolutely converging series. In detail, ${\int_X g^p \, d\mu < +\infty}$ gives ${g^p < +\infty}$ almost everywhere by (2) of Corollary 10 of the fifth post. Hence at almost every $x$ the series of the $f_n(x)$ converges, that is, the limit ${\lim_{N \to \infty} S_N(x)}$ exists. Thus $(S_N)$ converges almost everywhere, and we call ${S(x) := \lim_{N \to \infty} S_N(x)}$ at almost every ${x.}$

By the dominated convergence theorem in ${L^p,}$ Theorem 8 of the seventh post, ${S_N \to S}$ in ${L^p.}$ Indeed, ${S_N \to S}$ almost everywhere and ${|S_N| \le g \in L^p}$ almost everywhere for every ${N,}$ with ${p < +\infty.}$ $\blacksquare$

**Remark 18.** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space, let ${p \in [1, +\infty],}$ let ${(f_n) \subset L^p}$ and let ${f \colon X \to \mathbb{R}}$ be measurable. If ${f_n \to f}$ in ${L^p,}$ that is, ${\|f_n - f\|_p \to 0,}$ then ${f \in L^p.}$

**Proof (complete).** The sequence $(f_n)$ is Cauchy in ${L^p.}$ For all $n$ and ${m,}$

$$
\|f_n - f_m\|_p \le \|f_n - f\|_p + \|f - f_m\|_p,
$$

where the first term on the right tends to $0$ as ${n \to \infty}$ and the second as ${m \to \infty.}$ Indeed, this is Proposition 16 applied to ${f_n - f}$ and ${f - f_m,}$ which belong to $L^p$ as soon as their norms are finite. Also, for every ${\varepsilon > 0}$ there exists $N$ such that ${\|f_n - f\|_p < \varepsilon/2}$ for every ${n \ge N.}$ Hence, if ${n, m \ge N,}$ then ${\|f_n - f\|_p + \|f - f_m\|_p \le \varepsilon.}$

By the Riesz–Fischer theorem, Theorem 17, there exists ${g \in L^p}$ such that ${f_n \to g}$ in ${L^p.}$ By the uniqueness of limits, Remark 12 of the seventh post, ${g = f}$ almost everywhere, hence ${f \in L^p.}$ $\blacksquare$

### The spaces $W^{1,p}((a, b))$

**Example 19.** Let ${-\infty < a < b < +\infty}$ and consider the space $W^{1,p}((a, b))$ of Remark 19 of the previous post, with ${p \in [1, +\infty].}$ For ${f \in W^{1,p}((a, b))}$ let

$$
\|f\|_{W^{1,p}} := \|f\|_p + \|w\|_p,
$$

where $w$ is the weak derivative of ${f,}$ as in Definition 18 of the previous post. Then ${(W^{1,p}((a, b)), \|\cdot\|_{W^{1,p}})}$ is a Banach space.

**Remark 20.** Since ${\lambda((a, b)) < +\infty,}$ Corollary 13 gives ${L^p((a, b)) \subset L^1((a, b))}$ for every ${p \ge 1.}$ Hence

$$
W^{1,p}((a, b)) \subset W^{1,1}((a, b)) = \mathrm{AC}([a, b]),
$$

where $\mathrm{AC}([a, b])$ is the set of the absolutely continuous functions on $[a, b]$ of Definition 11 of the previous post, and the equality is Theorem 20 of the previous post: every function of $W^{1,p}((a, b))$ has an absolutely continuous representative.

**Example 21.** Let ${a,}$ $b$ and $p$ be as in Example 19. The set

$$
W_0^{1,p}((a, b)) = \{ f \in W^{1,p}((a, b)) : f(a) = f(b) = 0 \}
$$

is a subspace of ${W^{1,p}((a, b)).}$ In detail, $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ are the values at $a$ and $b$ of the absolutely continuous representative of $f$ of Remark 20.\
**(i)** The space ${(W_0^{1,p}((a, b)), \|\cdot\|_{W^{1,p}})}$ is a Banach space.

**(ii)** The space ${(W_0^{1,p}((a, b)), \|\cdot\|_{W_0^{1,p}})}$ is a Banach space, where for ${f \in W_0^{1,p}((a, b))}$

$$
\|f\|_{W_0^{1,p}} := \|w\|_p,
$$

with $w$ the weak derivative of ${f.}$ In fact, ${w = f'}$ almost everywhere, since ${f \in \mathrm{AC}([a, b]).}$
