The lecture recalls set theory and topology, and then begins real analysis with measurable spaces.

## Recap of set theory

The recap follows Hrbacek and Jech. Its aim is to distinguish between sets with infinitely many elements.

**Notation.** $A \subset B$ means that $A$ is contained in $B$ or equal to it, while $A \subsetneq B$ means that $A$ is strictly contained in $B.$

### Cardinality

**Definition 1.1 (Comparison of cardinalities).** Let $X$ and $Y$ be sets. We write ${\#X \le \#Y,}$ and say that the cardinality of $X$ is less than or equal to the cardinality of $Y,$ if there exists an injective map ${f \colon X \to Y.}$ We write ${\#X = \#Y}$ if there exists a bijective map ${f \colon X \to Y.}$ Finally, ${\#X < \#Y}$ means that ${\#X \le \#Y}$ and ${\#X \neq \#Y.}$

**Remark 1.2.** Let $X$ and $Y$ be sets.\
**(i)** If $X$ is nonempty, then ${\#X \le \#Y}$ if and only if there exists a surjective map ${f \colon Y \to X.}$\
**(ii)** ([Cantor–Bernstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schr%C3%B6der%E2%80%93Bernstein_theorem)) If ${\#X \le \#Y}$ and ${\#Y \le \#X,}$ then ${\#X = \#Y.}$

**Definition 1.3 (Countable and uncountable sets).** Let $X$ be a set.\
**(i)** $X$ is *countable* if $X$ is finite or ${\#X = \#\mathbb{N}.}$\
**(ii)** $X$ is *uncountable* if $X$ is infinite but ${\#\mathbb{N} < \#X.}$

**Example 1.4.** The natural numbers, the integers and the rationals have the same cardinality, ${\#\mathbb{N} = \#\mathbb{Z} = \#\mathbb{Q},}$ so $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ are countable. The real line is uncountable: ${\#\mathbb{R} > \#\mathbb{N}.}$

**Definition 1.5.** Define the set

$$
\overline{\mathbb{R}} = [-\infty, +\infty] := \mathbb{R} \cup \{+\infty\} \cup \{-\infty\},
$$

with the following operations in $\overline{\mathbb{R}}{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&a \in \mathbb{R} & &\Longrightarrow \quad a + \infty = +\infty, \quad a - \infty = -\infty, \\
&a > 0 & &\Longrightarrow \quad a \cdot (+\infty) = +\infty, \quad a \cdot (-\infty) = -\infty, \\
&a < 0 & &\Longrightarrow \quad a \cdot (+\infty) = -\infty, \quad a \cdot (-\infty) = +\infty, \\
& & &\phantom{\Longrightarrow \quad} 0 \cdot (+\infty) = 0 \cdot (-\infty) = 0.
\end{aligned}
$$

### The power set

**Definition 1.6 (Power set).** Let $X$ be a set. The *power set* of $X$ is the family of all subsets of $X,$

$$
\mathcal{P}(X) = \{ A : A \subset X \}.
$$

**Remark 1.7.** If ${\#X = n \in \mathbb{N},}$ then ${\#\mathcal{P}(X) = 2^n.}$

**Notation.** For a set $X,$ $2^X$ is the set of all functions from $X$ to the set with 2 elements $\{0, 1\}{:}$

$$
2^X := \{ f \colon X \to \{0, 1\} \}.
$$

**Proposition 1.8.** Let $X$ be a set.\
**(i)** ${\#\mathcal{P}(X) = \#2^X.}$\
**(ii)** ${\#\mathcal{P}(X) > \#X.}$ In particular, there exists no biggest infinity.

**Proof (complete).** (i) Define

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi \colon \mathcal{P}(X) &\to 2^X, \\
A &\mapsto f_A \colon X \to \{0, 1\}, \\
f_A(x) &:= \begin{cases} 1 & x \in A, \\ 0 & x \notin A. \end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

The map $\phi$ is a bijection.

(ii) Suppose, by contradiction, that ${\#\mathcal{P}(X) \le \#X.}$ By Remark 1.2 (i), applied to the nonempty set $\mathcal{P}(X),$ there exists a surjective map ${f \colon X \to \mathcal{P}(X).}$ Define

$$
U := \{ x \in X : x \notin f(x) \} \subset X.
$$

Since $f$ is surjective, there exists ${y \in X}$ with ${f(y) = U.}$ Is ${y \in U?}$\
Case 1. If ${y \in U,}$ then ${y \notin f(y)}$ by the definition of $U,$ and ${f(y) = U}$ gives ${y \notin U,}$ which is impossible.\
Case 2. If ${y \notin U = f(y),}$ then ${y \in U}$ by the definition of $U,$ which is impossible.

Hence ${\#\mathcal{P}(X) \le \#X}$ is false. To see that this gives ${\#\mathcal{P}(X) > \#X,}$ note that ${x \mapsto \{x\}}$ is injective, so ${\#X \le \#\mathcal{P}(X),}$ and ${\#X = \#\mathcal{P}(X)}$ would give ${\#\mathcal{P}(X) \le \#X.}$ $\blacksquare$

### Countable sets

**Notation.** A countable union is written $\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty} X_n,$ also $\bigcup_n X_n.$ A sequence is written $(x_n),$ and a family of sets is written ${\mathcal{F} = \{ X_\alpha \}_{\alpha \in A}.}$

**Proposition 1.9.** **(i)** ${\#\mathbb{R} = \#\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}) > \#\mathbb{N}.}$\
**(ii)** If $X$ and $Y$ are countable, then ${X \times Y = \{ (x, y) : x \in X, y \in Y \}}$ is countable.\
**(iii)** If ${X_0, X_1, X_2, \dots}$ is a sequence of sets such that $X_n$ is countable for every $n,$ then $\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty} X_n$ is countable: a countable union of countable sets is countable.

## Recap of topology

The recap follows Manetti. Topology gives an abstract theory of the open, closed and related subsets of some ambient space.

### Topological spaces

**Definition 1.10 (Topology).** Let $X$ be a set. A *topology* on $X$ is a family of subsets ${\tau \subset \mathcal{P}(X)}$ such that:\
**(i)** ${\emptyset, X \in \tau;}$\
**(ii)** for every ${\mathcal{F} = \{ U_\alpha \}_{\alpha \in A} \subset \tau,}$ the union $\bigcup_{\alpha \in A} U_\alpha$ belongs to $\tau;$\
**(iii)** if ${U_1, U_2, \dots, U_k \in \tau,}$ then ${\bigcap_{n=1}^{k} U_n \in \tau.}$\
The sets in $\tau$ are called *open*, and the couple $(X, \tau)$ is called a *topological space*.

**Example 1.11.** On $\mathbb{R}^n$ the *Euclidean topology* $\tau_E$ is defined as follows. We say that ${U \in \tau_E}$ if for every ${x \in U}$ there exists ${r > 0}$ with ${B_r(x) \subset U.}$ Here $B_r(x)$ is the open ball of centre $x$ and radius $r.$ Two other topologies on $\mathbb{R}^n$ are ${\tau = \{ \emptyset, \mathbb{R}^n \}}$ and ${\tau = \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^n).}$

**Definition 1.12 (Closed set, closure, interior, boundary).** Let $(X, \tau)$ be a topological space. A set ${V \subset X}$ is *closed* if ${X \setminus V}$ is open. For ${V \subset X{:}}$\
**(i)** the *closure* $\overline{V}$ of $V$ is the smallest closed set containing $V;$\
**(ii)** the *interior* ${\operatorname{int}(V) = \mathring{V}}$ of $V$ is the biggest open set contained in $V;$\
**(iii)** the *boundary* of $V$ is ${\partial V = \overline{V} \cap \overline{X \setminus V}.}$

**Definition 1.13 (Compact set).** Let $(X, \tau)$ be a topological space and ${K \subset X.}$ The set $K$ is *compact* if for every ${\mathcal{F} = \{ U_\alpha \}_{\alpha \in A} \subset \tau}$ such that ${\bigcup_{\alpha \in A} U_\alpha \supset K}$ there exists a finite subfamily ${\tilde{\mathcal{F}} = \{ V_1, \dots, V_m \} \subset \mathcal{F}}$ such that ${\bigcup_{n=1}^{m} V_n \supset K.}$

**Definition 1.14 (Precompact set).** A set ${V \subset X}$ is *precompact* if $\overline{V}$ is compact.

**Theorem 1.15 ([Heine–Borel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heine%E2%80%93Borel_theorem)).** A set ${K \subset \mathbb{R}^n}$ is compact if and only if $K$ is closed and bounded.

**Example 1.16 (Topology on $\overline{\mathbb{R}}$).** The following subsets of $\overline{\mathbb{R}}$ are open in $\overline{\mathbb{R}}{:}$ the intervals $(a, b)$ with ${a, b \in \mathbb{R};}$ every set that is open in $\mathbb{R},$ which is also defined to be open in $\overline{\mathbb{R}};$ and the sets $[-\infty, a)$ and $(a, +\infty]$ for every ${a \in \mathbb{R},}$ which are also defined to be open.

**Definition 1.17 (Continuous map).** Let $(X, \tau_X)$ and $(Y, \tau_Y)$ be two topological spaces and ${f \colon X \to Y.}$ The map $f$ is *continuous* if ${f^{-1}(A) \in \tau_X}$ for every ${A \in \tau_Y,}$ that is, if the preimage of any open set is open.

### Metric spaces

**Definition 1.18 (Metric space).** A *metric space* is a couple $(X, d)$ where $X$ is a set and $d$ is a *distance* on $X,$ that is, a function ${d \colon X \times X \to [0, +\infty)}$ such that, for all ${x, y, z \in X,}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&d(x, y) = 0 \iff x = y, \\
&d(x, y) = d(y, x), \\
&d(x, y) \le d(x, z) + d(z, y).
\end{aligned}
$$

**Definition 1.19 (Topology induced by a distance).** Let $(X, d)$ be a metric space. For ${x \in X}$ and ${r > 0,}$ define

$$
B_r(x) := \{ y \in X : d(x, y) < r \}.
$$

A set $U$ in $X$ is *open* if for every ${x \in U}$ there exists ${r > 0}$ such that ${B_r(x) \subset U.}$ These open sets form the *topology induced by the distance* $d.$

A sequence ${(x_n) \subset X}$ *converges* to ${x_0 \in X,}$ written ${x_n \to x_0,}$ if ${\lim_{n \to \infty} d(x_n, x_0) = 0.}$

**Lemma 1.20.** Let $(X, d)$ be a metric space.\
**(i)** A set ${V \subset X}$ is closed if and only if $V$ is *sequentially closed*: if ${(x_n) \subset V}$ and ${x_n \to x_0,}$ then ${x_0 \in V.}$\
**(ii)** A set ${K \subset X}$ is compact if and only if $K$ is *sequentially compact*: every ${(x_n) \subset K}$ has a subsequence $(x_{n_k})$ such that ${x_{n_k} \to x_0 \in K.}$\
**(iii)** If $V$ is closed, $K$ is compact and ${V \subset K,}$ then $V$ is compact.

**Lemma 1.21.** Let $(X, d_X)$ and $(Y, d_Y)$ be two metric spaces and ${f \colon X \to Y.}$ The following are equivalent.\
**(i)** $f$ is continuous.\
**(ii)** For every ${x_0 \in X}$ and every ${\varepsilon > 0}$ there exists ${\delta > 0}$ such that ${d_X(x, x_0) < \delta}$ implies ${d_Y\big(f(x), f(x_0)\big) < \varepsilon.}$\
**(iii)** For every ${x_0 \in X}$ and every ${(x_n) \subset X}$ with ${x_n \to x_0,}$ that is, ${d_X(x_n, x_0) \to 0,}$ the sequence ${\big(f(x_n)\big) \subset Y}$ is such that ${f(x_n) \to f(x_0),}$ that is, ${d_Y\big(f(x_n), f(x_0)\big) \to 0.}$

## Measurable spaces

Real analysis begins here, following Folland.

### σ-algebras

**Definition 1.22 ($\sigma$-algebra, measurable space).** Let $X$ be a set. A *measurable space* is a couple $(X, \mathcal{M})$ where $\mathcal{M}$ is a *$\sigma$-algebra*, that is:\
**(i)** ${\mathcal{M} \subset \mathcal{P}(X);}$\
**(ii)** ${\emptyset \in \mathcal{M};}$\
**(iii)** for every ${A \in \mathcal{M},}$ ${X \setminus A \in \mathcal{M};}$\
**(iv)** for every ${(A_n) \subset \mathcal{M},}$ ${\bigcup_n A_n \in \mathcal{M}.}$

**Example 1.23.** For every set $X,$ $\mathcal{P}(X)$ and ${\mathcal{M} = \{ \emptyset, X \}}$ are $\sigma$-algebras. Most of the times a topology is not a $\sigma$-algebra, for example the Euclidean topology.

**Remark 1.24.** Let $(X, \mathcal{M})$ be a measurable space.\
**(i)** If ${E, F \in \mathcal{M},}$ then ${E \cap F \in \mathcal{M}}$ and ${E \setminus F \in \mathcal{M}.}$\
**(ii)** If ${(E_n) \subset \mathcal{M},}$ then ${\bigcap_n E_n \in \mathcal{M}.}$

**Proof (sketch).** Intersections are written through complements and unions:

$$
E \cap F = X \setminus \big( X \setminus (E \cap F) \big) = X \setminus \big( (X \setminus E) \cup (X \setminus F) \big). \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

**Definition 1.25 (Restricted $\sigma$-algebra).** Let $(X, \mathcal{M})$ be a measurable space and ${\Omega \subset X.}$ The *restricted $\sigma$-algebra* is

$$
\mathcal{M}_\Omega = \mathcal{M}|_\Omega := \{ E \cap \Omega : E \in \mathcal{M} \}.
$$

One checks that $(\Omega, \mathcal{M}_\Omega)$ is a measurable space. Indeed, ${\emptyset = \emptyset \cap \Omega,}$ ${\Omega \setminus (E \cap \Omega) = (X \setminus E) \cap \Omega}$ and ${\bigcup_n (E_n \cap \Omega) = \big(\bigcup_n E_n\big) \cap \Omega.}$

### Generated and Borel σ-algebras

**Theorem 1.26 (Generation of $\sigma$-algebras).** Let $X$ be a set and ${\mathcal{F} \subset \mathcal{P}(X).}$ There exists a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{M}$ on $X$ such that:\
**(i)** ${\mathcal{F} \subset \mathcal{M};}$\
**(ii)** for every $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{Q}$ on $X$ with ${\mathcal{F} \subset \mathcal{Q},}$ ${\mathcal{M} \subset \mathcal{Q}.}$\
In words, $\mathcal{M}$ is the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing $\mathcal{F}.$ It is denoted $\sigma_0(\mathcal{F}).$

**Proof (sketch).** Consider

$$
W := \{ \mathcal{Q} \subset \mathcal{P}(X) : \mathcal{Q} \text{ is a } \sigma\text{-algebra}, \ \mathcal{F} \subset \mathcal{Q} \}.
$$

Then ${W \neq \emptyset,}$ as ${\mathcal{P}(X) \in W.}$ Define

$$
\mathcal{M} := \bigcap \{ \mathcal{Q} : \mathcal{Q} \in W \}. \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

**Definition 1.27 (Borel $\sigma$-algebra).** Let $(X, \tau)$ be a topological space. The *Borel $\sigma$-algebra* is $\sigma_0(\tau),$ denoted $\mathcal{B}(X).$

**Remark 1.28.** For $\mathbb{R}$ with the Euclidean topology,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}) &= \sigma_0\big( \{ \text{open sets} \} \big) \\
&= \sigma_0\big( \{ (a, b) : a < b \} \big) \\
&= \sigma_0\big( \{ [a, b] : a < b \} \big) \\
&= \sigma_0\big( \{ (a, +\infty) : a \in \mathbb{R} \} \big).
\end{aligned}
$$

Similarly,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{B}(\overline{\mathbb{R}}) &= \sigma_0\big( \{ (a, +\infty] : a \in \mathbb{R} \} \big), \\
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^N) &= \sigma_0\big( \{ \text{open rectangles} \} \big),
\end{aligned}
$$

where an *open rectangle* is a set ${(a_1, b_1) \times (a_2, b_2) \times \dots \times (a_N, b_N).}$
