The study of the topology of normed spaces of infinite dimension ends with separability.

## Topology in infinite dimensions (conclusion)

### Separability

**Definition 13.1 (Separable metric space).** A metric space ${(X, d)}$ is said to be *separable* if there exists ${D \subset X}$ such that $D$ is dense and countable, as in Definition 12.8 and Definition 1.3.

**Example 13.2.** The set ${\mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}}$ is dense by Example 12.10, and it is countable by Example 1.4. Hence ${\mathbb{R},}$ with the distance ${d(x, y) = |x - y|,}$ is separable.

**Remark 13.3.** Let ${(X, d)}$ be a metric space and let ${D \subset X.}$ Then $D$ is dense if and only if ${U \cap D \neq \emptyset}$ for every nonempty open set ${U \subset X.}$

**Proof (sketch).** Only the implication from left to right is proved. Let $D$ be dense, let ${U \subset X}$ be open with ${U \neq \emptyset,}$ and take ${x \in U.}$ Since $U$ is open, there exists ${r > 0}$ such that ${B_r(x) \subset U,}$ by Definition 1.19. Since $D$ is dense, by Definition 12.8 there exists ${y \in B_r(x) \cap D,}$ and ${B_r(x) \cap D \subset U \cap D.}$ Hence ${U \cap D \neq \emptyset.}$ $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 13.4.** Let ${-\infty < a < b < +\infty,}$ let $N$ be a positive integer and let ${U \subset \mathbb{R}^N}$ be open, with the Lebesgue measure as in Example 10.8 (i).\
**(1)** The space ${(C([a, b]), \|\cdot\|_\infty)}$ of Example 10.5 (iii) is separable. The idea is the Stone–Weierstrass theorem in Remark 12.15, by which the polynomials are dense in ${C([a, b]){:}}$ the polynomials ${a_0 + a_1 x + \dots + a_k x^k}$ with ${a_i \in \mathbb{Q}}$ form a dense and countable subset of ${C([a, b]).}$\
**(2)** For every ${p \in [1, +\infty),}$ the space ${(L^p(U), \|\cdot\|_p)}$ is separable.\
**(3)** The space ${(L^\infty(U), \|\cdot\|_\infty)}$ is not separable.

**Proof (sketch).** Only (3) is proved, in the case ${N = 1}$ and ${U = \mathbb{R},}$ that is, for ${L^\infty(\mathbb{R}).}$ For ${\alpha > 0}$ let

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_\alpha &:= \chi_{(-\alpha, \alpha)} \in L^\infty(\mathbb{R}), \\
U_\alpha &:= B_{1/2}(f_\alpha) \subset L^\infty(\mathbb{R}).
\end{aligned}
$$

If ${\alpha \neq \beta,}$ then ${\|f_\alpha - f_\beta\|_\infty = 1,}$ as in Figure 13.1.

Suppose, by contradiction, that there exists ${D = \{ u_m : m \in \mathbb{N} \} \subset L^\infty(\mathbb{R})}$ dense and countable. By Remark 13.3, ${D \cap U_\alpha \neq \emptyset}$ for every ${\alpha > 0.}$ That is, for every ${\alpha > 0}$ there exists ${m_\alpha \in \mathbb{N}}$ such that ${u_{m_\alpha} \in U_\alpha,}$ so that there is a function

$$
\phi \colon (0, +\infty) \to \mathbb{N}, \qquad \alpha \mapsto m_\alpha.
$$

The function $\phi$ is injective. Let ${\alpha \neq \beta}$ and suppose that ${m_\alpha = m_\beta.}$ Then ${u_{m_\alpha} = u_{m_\beta}}$ belongs to $U_\alpha$ and to ${U_\beta,}$ so that ${U_\alpha \cap U_\beta \neq \emptyset,}$ which is impossible. Hence ${m_\alpha \neq m_\beta.}$

Therefore ${\#(0, +\infty) \le \#\mathbb{N}}$ by Definition 1.1, a contradiction. $\blacksquare$

## Linear operators

**Definition 13.5 (Linear operator, bounded operator).** Let ${(X, \|\cdot\|_X)}$ and ${(Y, \|\cdot\|_Y)}$ be normed spaces.

**(i)** A *linear operator* is a map ${T \colon X \to Y}$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(\alpha x) &= \alpha Tx && \text{for all } \alpha \in \mathbb{R} \text{ and } x \in X, \\
T(x + y) &= Tx + Ty && \text{for all } x, y \in X.
\end{aligned}
$$

**(ii)** A linear operator $T$ is said to be *bounded* if there exists ${C > 0}$ such that ${\|Tx\|_Y \le C \|x\|_X}$ for every ${x \in X.}$

**Remark 13.6.** Let $N$ and $M$ be positive integers. If ${T \colon \mathbb{R}^N \to \mathbb{R}^M}$ is linear, then $T$ is represented by a matrix, and $T$ is continuous.

**Example 13.7.** Let ${-\infty < a < b < +\infty.}$ The linear operator $T$ from ${(C^1([a, b]), \|\cdot\|_\infty)}$ to ${(C([a, b]), \|\cdot\|_\infty)}$ given by ${Tf = f'}$ is not continuous.

For Example 13.7, recall that, by Lemma 1.21, a map ${T \colon X \to Y}$ between normed spaces is continuous if and only if ${Tx_m \to Tx_0}$ whenever ${x_m \to x_0}$ in ${X.}$ We find ${(f_m) \subset C^1([a, b])}$ such that ${f_m \to 0}$ with respect to ${\|\cdot\|_\infty,}$ while the derivatives ${Tf_m = f_m'}$ do not converge to the derivative of the function ${0,}$ which is ${0,}$ in ${\|\cdot\|_\infty.}$

Suppose that ${a < 0 < b,}$ as in Figure 13.2, and let $m$ be an integer with ${1/m < b.}$ Let ${f_m \in C^1([a, b])}$ vanish outside ${(0, 1/m),}$ with ${0 \le f_m \le 1/m}$ and ${f_m(1/(2m)) = 1/m.}$

(i) The norms satisfy ${\|f_m\|_\infty \le 1/m,}$ which tends to ${0,}$ so that ${f_m \to 0}$ in ${\|\cdot\|_\infty.}$ (ii) By the [mean value theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_value_theorem), there exists ${x_m \in (0, 1/(2m))}$ such that ${f_m'(x_m) = 2.}$ Hence ${\|f_m'\|_\infty \ge 2}$ for every ${m,}$ so that $\|f_m'\|_\infty$ does not tend to ${0,}$ and $T$ is not continuous.

A map $T$ from a normed space $X$ to a normed space $Y$ is *Lipschitz* if there exists ${L > 0}$ such that ${\|Tx - Ty\|_Y \le L \|x - y\|_X}$ for all ${x, y \in X.}$ It is *continuous at* $0$ if for every ${\varepsilon > 0}$ there exists ${\delta > 0}$ such that

$$
\|x\|_X < \delta \implies \|Tx - T0\|_Y < \varepsilon.
$$

**Theorem 13.8 (Characterization of continuity).** Let ${T \colon (X, \|\cdot\|_X) \to (Y, \|\cdot\|_Y)}$ be linear. The following are equivalent.\
**(1)** $T$ is bounded.\
**(2)** $T$ is Lipschitz.\
**(3)** $T$ is continuous.\
**(4)** $T$ is continuous at ${0.}$

**Proof (complete).** We prove that (1) implies (2), that (2) implies (3), that (3) implies (4) and that (4) implies (1). Suppose (1), with the constant $C$ of Definition 13.5 (ii). Then, for all ${x, y \in X,}$

$$
\|Tx - Ty\|_Y = \|T(x - y)\|_Y \le C \|x - y\|_X,
$$

where the equality is the linearity of $T$ and the inequality is (1) applied to ${x - y.}$ Hence (2) holds with ${L = C.}$

Suppose (2), and let ${(x_m) \subset X}$ be such that ${x_m \to x_0}$ in ${X.}$ Then, by (2),

$$
\|Tx_m - Tx_0\|_Y \le L \|x_m - x_0\|_X \longrightarrow 0,
$$

so that ${Tx_m \to Tx_0,}$ and $T$ is continuous by Lemma 1.21. Next, (3) implies (4) by Lemma 1.21 (ii) at the point ${x_0 = 0.}$

Suppose (4). Since ${T0 = 0}$ by linearity, (4) means that for every ${\varepsilon > 0}$ there exists ${\delta > 0}$ such that

$$
\|x\|_X \le \delta \implies \|Tx\|_Y \le \varepsilon.
$$

In detail, the strict inequalities of the definition of continuity at $0$ give this with ${\delta/2}$ in place of ${\delta.}$ Take ${\varepsilon = 1.}$ There is ${\delta > 0}$ such that ${\|x\|_X \le \delta}$ implies ${\|Tx\|_Y \le 1.}$ If ${x = 0,}$ then ${\|Tx\|_Y = \|T0\|_Y = \|0\|_Y = 0 = \|x\|_X.}$ If ${x \neq 0,}$ the vector ${\delta x/\|x\|_X}$ has norm ${\delta,}$ hence, by (4),

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\biggl\| T\biggl( \delta \frac{x}{\|x\|_X} \biggr) \biggr\|_Y \le 1, \qquad T\biggl( \delta \frac{x}{\|x\|_X} \biggr) = \frac{\delta}{\|x\|_X} \, Tx, \\
&\Longrightarrow \quad \biggl\| \frac{\delta}{\|x\|_X} \, Tx \biggr\|_Y \le 1 \\
&\Longrightarrow \quad \frac{\delta}{\|x\|_X} \, \|Tx\|_Y \le 1 \\
&\Longrightarrow \quad \|Tx\|_Y \le \frac{1}{\delta} \, \|x\|_X,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the equality in the first line is the linearity of ${T.}$ The last inequality holds for every ${x \in X,}$ the case ${x = 0}$ included, that is, $T$ is bounded. $\blacksquare$

**Definition 13.9 (Dual space).** Let ${(X, \|\cdot\|_X)}$ and ${(Y, \|\cdot\|_Y)}$ be normed spaces.\
**(i)** The set of the linear and continuous operators ${T \colon X \to Y}$ is denoted ${\mathcal{L}(X, Y).}$ We observe that $\mathcal{L}(X, Y)$ is a vector space, with ${(T + S)(x) = Tx + Sx}$ and ${(\alpha T)(x) = \alpha Tx}$ for ${\alpha \in \mathbb{R}.}$\
**(ii)** We write ${\mathcal{L}(X) := \mathcal{L}(X, X).}$\
**(iii)** The set ${X^* := \mathcal{L}(X, \mathbb{R}),}$ where $\mathbb{R}$ has the norm ${|\cdot|,}$ is the set of the linear continuous operators ${T \colon X \to \mathbb{R},}$ the *functionals on* ${X.}$ It is also denoted ${X',}$ and it is called the *dual space* of ${X.}$

**(iv)** On $\mathcal{L}(X, Y)$ we define

$$
\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}(X, Y)} := \sup_{\|x\|_X \le 1} \|Tx\|_Y,
$$

also written $\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}}$ or ${\|T\|.}$ Notice that ${(\mathcal{L}(X, Y), \|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}(X, Y)})}$ is a normed space, and that, if ${T \in X^*,}$ then

$$
\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}} = \sup_{\|x\|_X \le 1} |Tx|.
$$

**Proposition 13.10 (Characterization of $\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}}$).** Let ${(X, \|\cdot\|_X)}$ and ${(Y, \|\cdot\|_Y)}$ be normed spaces with ${X \neq \{0\},}$ and let ${T \in \mathcal{L}(X, Y).}$ Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}} &= \sup_{\|x\|_X = 1} \|Tx\|_Y = \sup_{\substack{x \in X \\ x \neq 0}} \frac{\|Tx\|_Y}{\|x\|_X} \\
&= \inf \{ M > 0 : \|Tx\|_Y \le M \|x\|_X \ \text{for every } x \in X \}.
\end{aligned}
$$

In words, $\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}}$ is the least constant that can be put in the definition of a bounded operator, Definition 13.5 (ii). In particular, ${\|Tx\|_Y \le \|T\|_{\mathcal{L}} \|x\|_X}$ for every ${x \in X.}$

**Proof (complete).** By the definition of ${\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}},}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}} &= \sup_{\|x\|_X \le 1} \|Tx\|_Y \\
&\ge \sup_{\|x\|_X = 1} \|Tx\|_Y \\
&\ge \sup_{\substack{x \in X \\ x \neq 0}} \frac{\|Tx\|_Y}{\|x\|_X} =: M_0 \\
&\ge \inf \{ M > 0 : \|Tx\|_Y \le M \|x\|_X \ \text{for every } x \in X \} =: M_1.
\end{aligned}
$$

The first inequality restricts the set where the supremum is taken. For the second, if ${x \neq 0,}$ then ${x/\|x\|_X}$ has norm $1$ and, by linearity,

$$
\frac{\|Tx\|_Y}{\|x\|_X} = \biggl\| T\biggl( \frac{x}{\|x\|_X} \biggr) \biggr\|_Y \le \sup_{\|z\|_X = 1} \|Tz\|_Y.
$$

For the third, the definition of $M_0$ gives ${\|Tx\|_Y/\|x\|_X \le M_0}$ for every ${x \neq 0.}$ Hence ${\|Tx\|_Y \le M_0 \|x\|_X}$ for every ${x \in X,}$ so $M_0$ is a competitor in the computation of that infimum, and ${M_1 \le M_0.}$ In detail, if ${M_0 = 0,}$ then every ${M > 0}$ is a competitor and ${M_1 = 0.}$

The last step is ${M_1 \ge \|T\|_{\mathcal{L}}.}$ Let ${M > 0}$ be any competitor in the infimum, that is, ${\|Tx\|_Y \le M \|x\|_X}$ for every ${x \in X.}$ Then, for every $x$ with ${\|x\|_X \le 1,}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\|Tx\|_Y \le M \|x\|_X \le M \\
&\Longrightarrow \quad \|T\|_{\mathcal{L}} = \sup_{\|x\|_X \le 1} \|Tx\|_Y \le M.
\end{aligned}
$$

Taking the infimum over $M$ gives ${\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}} \le M_1.}$ $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 13.11.** Let ${(X, \|\cdot\|_X)}$ be a normed space and ${(Y, \|\cdot\|_Y)}$ a Banach space, as in Definition 10.4 (ii). Then ${(\mathcal{L}(X, Y), \|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}(X, Y)})}$ is a Banach space. In particular, $X^*$ is a Banach space.

**Definition 13.12 (Kernel, range).** Let ${T \colon (X, \|\cdot\|_X) \to (Y, \|\cdot\|_Y)}$ be linear.\
**(i)** The *kernel* of $T$ is ${\operatorname{Ker}(T) = \{ x \in X : Tx = 0 \}.}$\
**(ii)** The *range*, or image, of $T$ is ${R(T) = \{ Tx : x \in X \}.}$

**Remark 13.13.** Let ${T \in \mathcal{L}(X, Y).}$ Then $\operatorname{Ker}(T)$ is a closed subspace of ${X,}$ but $R(T)$ is not always closed in ${Y.}$

**Remark 13.14.** Let ${(X, \|\cdot\|_X)}$ and ${(Y, \|\cdot\|_Y)}$ be normed spaces and let ${T \colon X \to Y}$ be linear.\
**(i)** $T$ is injective if and only if ${\operatorname{Ker}(T) = \{0\}.}$\
**(ii)** If $T$ is bijective, then its inverse ${T^{-1} \colon Y \to X}$ exists and is linear.\
**(iii)** If ${T \in \mathcal{L}(X, Y)}$ is bijective, it is not always true that ${T^{-1}}$ is continuous.

**Definition 13.15 (Isomorphism, isometry, isometric isomorphism).** Let ${(X, \|\cdot\|_X)}$ and ${(Y, \|\cdot\|_Y)}$ be normed spaces.\
**(i)** A map ${T \colon X \to Y}$ is an *isomorphism* of normed spaces if ${T \in \mathcal{L}(X, Y),}$ $T$ is bijective and ${T^{-1} \in \mathcal{L}(Y, X).}$\
**(ii)** A map ${T \in \mathcal{L}(X, Y)}$ is an *isometry* if ${\|Tx\|_Y = \|x\|_X}$ for every ${x \in X.}$ Notice that an isometry is not required to be surjective.\
**(iii)** A map ${T \colon X \to Y}$ is an *isometric isomorphism* if $T$ is an isomorphism and an isometry. Notice that in this case also ${T^{-1}}$ is an isometric isomorphism.

**Remark 13.16.** Let ${T \in \mathcal{L}(X, Y)}$ be an isometry.\
**(i)** $T$ is injective.\
**(ii)** $T$ is an isometric isomorphism if and only if $T$ is surjective.

**Definition 13.17 (Embedding).** Let ${(Y, \|\cdot\|_Y)}$ be a normed space and let ${X \subset Y}$ be a subspace such that ${(X, \|\cdot\|_X)}$ is a normed space. Consider

$$
J \colon (X, \|\cdot\|_X) \to (Y, \|\cdot\|_Y), \qquad x \mapsto x,
$$

called the identity, the immersion or the injection of $X$ into ${Y.}$ The map $J$ is called an *embedding* if it is continuous.

By Definition 13.17 and Theorem 13.8, $J$ is an embedding if and only if $J$ is bounded. That is, there exists ${C > 0}$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\|Jx\|_Y \le C \|x\|_X \quad \text{for every } x \in X, \\
\text{that is,} \quad &\|x\|_Y \le C \|x\|_X \quad \text{for every } x \in X.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Example 13.18.** Let ${(\Omega, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space with ${\mu(\Omega) < +\infty,}$ and let ${1 \le p \le q \le +\infty.}$ By the Hölder inequality, ${L^q(\Omega) \subset L^p(\Omega),}$ as in Corollary 10.13, and we can consider

$$
J \colon L^q(\Omega) \to L^p(\Omega), \qquad f \mapsto f.
$$

The Hölder inequality, Proposition 10.12, gives a constant ${C > 0}$ such that ${\|f\|_p \le C \|f\|_q}$ for every ${f \in L^q(\Omega).}$ That is, $J$ is continuous: $L^q(\Omega)$ embeds into ${L^p(\Omega).}$

## Baire's lemma

A metric space ${(X, d)}$ is *complete* if every Cauchy sequence converges in ${X,}$ where Cauchy sequences are defined as in Definition 10.4 (i) with ${d(x_n, x_m)}$ in place of ${\|x_n - x_m\|.}$

**Theorem 13.19 (Baire's lemma).** Let ${(X, d)}$ be a complete metric space.\
**(1)** If ${(U_m)_{m \ge 1}}$ is a sequence of open dense sets in ${X,}$ then their intersection is dense in ${X.}$\
**(2)** If ${(C_m)_{m \ge 1}}$ is a sequence of closed sets in $X$ such that ${\operatorname{int}(C_m) = \emptyset}$ for every ${m,}$ then their union has empty interior.

In Theorem 13.19, (1) and (2) are equivalent: with ${C_m = X \setminus U_m}$ for every ${m,}$

$$
\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty} C_m = X \setminus \bigcap_{m=1}^{\infty} U_m.
$$

Indeed, by Remark 13.3 a set ${D \subset X}$ is dense if and only if ${\operatorname{int}(X \setminus D) = \emptyset,}$ since both mean that every nonempty open set meets ${D.}$ Moreover, $C_m$ is closed if and only if $U_m$ is open.

**Corollary 13.20.** Let ${(X, d)}$ be a nonempty complete metric space and let ${(C_m)_{m \ge 1}}$ be a sequence of closed sets whose union is ${X.}$ Then there exists $m_0$ such that ${\operatorname{int}(C_{m_0}) \neq \emptyset.}$

**Proof (complete).** We have

$$
\operatorname{int}\Big( \bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty} C_m \Big) = \operatorname{int}(X) \neq \emptyset.
$$

Hence, by Theorem 13.19 (2), not every element $C_m$ of the sequence has empty interior. $\blacksquare$

**Proof (complete).** We prove Theorem 13.19 (1); then (2) holds by the equivalence of (1) and (2). Let ${(U_m)_{m \ge 1}}$ be open and dense, and take ${O \subset X}$ open with ${O \neq \emptyset.}$ By Remark 13.3, the goal is

$$
\bigcap_{m=1}^{\infty} U_m \cap O \neq \emptyset.
$$

(i) The set ${U_1 \cap O}$ is open, and it is nonempty because $U_1$ is dense, by Remark 13.3. Hence there exist ${x_1 \in X}$ and ${r_1 \in (0, 1/2)}$ such that the closed ball ${\overline{B}_{r_1}(x_1) = \{ y \in X : d(x_1, y) \le r_1 \}}$ is contained in ${O \cap U_1.}$

(ii) The set ${U_2 \cap B_{r_1}(x_1)}$ is open, and it is nonempty because $U_2$ is dense. Hence, as in Figure 13.3, there exist ${x_2 \in X}$ and ${r_2 \in (0, 1/4)}$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\overline{B}_{r_2}(x_2) &\subset B_{r_1}(x_1) \cap U_2 \\
&\subset O \cap U_1 \cap U_2.
\end{aligned}
$$

(iii) Iterating, we get points ${x_m \in X}$ and radii ${r_m \in (0, 1/2^m),}$ and finding this sequence relies on the [axiom of choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_of_choice). For every ${m \ge 2,}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\overline{B}_{r_m}(x_m) &\subset B_{r_{m-1}}(x_{m-1}) \cap U_m \\
&\subset O \cap U_1 \cap U_2 \cap \dots \cap U_m \\
&= O \cap \bigcap_{k=1}^{m} U_k.
\end{aligned}
$$

The sequence $(x_m)$ is Cauchy. For all ${n, m \ge N}$ we have ${x_n, x_m \in B_{r_N}(x_N),}$ hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(x_n, x_m) &\le 2 r_N \\
&< \frac{2}{2^N}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Since ${2/2^N \to 0}$ as ${N \to \infty,}$ the sequence $(x_m)$ is Cauchy. By completeness, there exists ${x_\infty \in X}$ such that ${x_m \to x_\infty.}$ We claim that $x_\infty$ belongs to $O$ and to every ${U_m,}$ so that the set of the goal is nonempty. For every $N$ and every ${m \ge N}$ we have ${x_m \in \overline{B}_{r_N}(x_N),}$ as in Figure 13.4. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_\infty &\in \overline{B}_{r_N}(x_N) \\
&\subset O \cap \bigcap_{k=1}^{N} U_k \qquad \text{for every } N.
\end{aligned}
$$
