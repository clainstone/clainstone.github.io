The Lebesgue integral of the previous post is now applied to sequences of measurable functions, and three theorems compare the integral of their limit with the limit of their integrals. The integral then defines the spaces ${L^p.}$

## Three most important theorems

**Theorem 1 (Fatou's lemma).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space and let $(f_n)$ be a sequence of measurable functions ${f_n \colon X \to [0, +\infty].}$ Let ${f \colon X \to [0, +\infty]}$ be given by ${f(x) = \varliminf_{n \to \infty} f_n(x),}$ written ${f = \varliminf_n f_n.}$ Then

$$
\varliminf_n \int_X f_n \, d\mu \ge \int_X f \, d\mu, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad \varliminf_n \int_X f_n \, d\mu \ge \int_X \varliminf_n f_n \, d\mu.
$$

**Proof (sketch).** Let

$$
s = \sum_{k=1}^{m} a_k \chi_{A_k}
$$

be a simple function such that ${0 \le s \le f,}$ written as in Definition 16 of the fourth post. Only the case ${0 < a_k < +\infty}$ for every ${k = 1, \dots, m}$ is treated. Let ${t \in (0, 1).}$ For every ${k = 1, \dots, m}$ and every ${n \in \mathbb{N}}$ define

$$
B_{k,n} := \{ x \in A_k : f_j(x) \ge t a_k \ \text{for every } j \ge n \} \subset A_k,
$$

as in Figure 1. These sets satisfy ${B_{k,n} \subset B_{k,n+1}}$ for every $n$ and every ${k.}$ Indeed, $B_{k,n}$ is the intersection of $A_k$ with the measurable sets ${\{ f_j \ge t a_k \}}$ for ${j \ge n,}$ and $B_{k,n+1}$ leaves out the set with ${j = n.}$

**Figure 1.** The drawing of the proof, with an instance on an interval $X$ and ${\mu = \lambda{:}}$ the function ${f,}$ the level $a_k$ of $s$ over the set ${A_k,}$ the dotted level ${t a_k,}$ the function $f_n$ and, faint, the next functions $f_j$ with ${j > n.}$ On the axis, the set ${B_{k,n},}$ under hatched rectangles of height ${t a_k;}$ the sliders set $n$ and ${t.}$

Let ${x \in A_k.}$ Then ${f(x) = \varliminf_n f_n(x) \ge s(x) = a_k > t a_k.}$ Hence there exists ${n_x \in \mathbb{N}}$ such that ${f_j(x) \ge t a_k}$ for every ${j \ge n_x.}$ In other words, for every ${x \in A_k}$ there exists $n_x$ such that ${x \in B_{k, n_x},}$ so that

$$
\bigcup_n B_{k,n} = A_k
$$

and, by the continuity of measures, Proposition 6 (i) of the second post,

$$
\mu(A_k) = \lim_{n \to +\infty} \mu(B_{k,n}).
$$

For every ${n,}$ by monotonicity, and since ${f_n \ge t a_k}$ on ${B_{k,n},}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_X f_n \, d\mu &\ge \sum_{k=1}^{m} \int_{B_{k,n}} f_n \, d\mu \\
&\ge \sum_{k=1}^{m} \int_{B_{k,n}} t a_k \, d\mu \\
&= \sum_{k=1}^{m} t a_k \, \mu(B_{k,n}).
\end{aligned}
$$

Indeed, the sets ${B_{1,n}, \dots, B_{m,n}}$ are pairwise disjoint, since each $B_{k,n}$ is contained in ${A_k,}$ so the first inequality follows from (6) and (3) of Remark 4 of the previous post. Taking the lower limit as ${n \to \infty}$ and using the limit above,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varliminf_{n \to \infty} \int_X f_n \, d\mu &\ge \sum_{k=1}^{m} t a_k \varliminf_n \mu(B_{k,n}) \\
&= \sum_{k=1}^{m} t a_k \, \mu(A_k) \\
&= t \sum_{k=1}^{m} a_k \, \mu(A_k) \\
&= t \int_X s \, d\mu.
\end{aligned}
$$

Taking the supremum over the simple functions $s$ with ${0 \le s \le f}$ gives, by Definition 3 of the previous post,

$$
\varliminf_n \int_X f_n \, d\mu \ge t \int_X f \, d\mu,
$$

and letting ${t \to 1^-}$ gives

$$
\varliminf_n \int_X f_n \, d\mu \ge \int_X f \, d\mu. \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

**Remark 2.** In Theorem 1 the assumption ${f_n \colon X \to [0, +\infty]}$ is needed. Otherwise, on $\mathbb{R}$ with the Lebesgue measure ${\lambda,}$ let ${f_n = -n \chi_{[0, 1/n]}}$ for every ${n \ge 1,}$ as in Figure 2. Then ${\int_{\mathbb{R}} f_n \, d\lambda = -1}$ for every ${n,}$ and ${f_n \to 0}$ almost everywhere, so that, with ${f = 0,}$

$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}} f \, d\lambda = 0 \not\le \varliminf_n \int_{\mathbb{R}} f_n \, d\lambda = -1.
$$

**Remark 3 (Reverse Fatou's lemma).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space, let $(f_n)$ be a sequence of measurable functions ${f_n \colon X \to [0, +\infty),}$ and let ${f = \varlimsup_n f_n.}$ Assume that there exists a measurable ${g \colon X \to [0, +\infty]}$ such that ${g \in \mathcal{L}^1}$ and ${g \ge f_n}$ almost everywhere for every ${n.}$ Then

$$
\varlimsup_n \int_X f_n \, d\mu \le \int_X f \, d\mu.
$$

**Proof (sketch).** Let ${h_n = g - f_n,}$ as in Figure 3, so that ${h_n \ge 0}$ almost everywhere, and

$$
\varliminf_n h_n = g - \varlimsup_n f_n = g - f.
$$

Fatou's lemma, Theorem 1, applied to $(h_n)$ gives

$$
\varliminf_n \int_X h_n \, d\mu \ge \int_X (g - f) \, d\mu,
$$

where

$$
\varliminf_n \int_X h_n \, d\mu = \int_X g \, d\mu - \varlimsup_n \int_X f_n \, d\mu.
$$

In detail, ${\int_X (g - f) \, d\mu = \int_X g \, d\mu - \int_X f \, d\mu,}$ and ${\int_X g \, d\mu < +\infty}$ because ${g \in \mathcal{L}^1.}$ Subtracting ${\int_X g \, d\mu}$ from both sides gives the claim. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4 (Monotone convergence, Beppo Levi).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space and let $(f_n)$ be a sequence of measurable functions ${f_n \colon X \to [0, +\infty].}$ Assume that ${f_{n+1} \ge f_n}$ almost everywhere for every ${n,}$ as in Figure 4. Let ${f \colon X \to [0, +\infty]}$ be such that ${f = \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n}$ almost everywhere, that is, ${f(x) = \sup_n f_n(x)}$ for almost every ${x.}$ Then

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_X f_n \, d\mu = \int_X f \, d\mu, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad \sup_n \int_X f_n \, d\mu = \int_X \sup_n f_n \, d\mu.
$$

**Proof (complete).** We have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_X f \, d\mu &\le \varliminf_n \int_X f_n \, d\mu \le \varlimsup_n \int_X f_n \, d\mu \\
&\le \varlimsup_n \int_X f \, d\mu = \int_X f \, d\mu,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the first inequality is Fatou's lemma, Theorem 1, and the third is monotonicity, as ${f_n \le f}$ almost everywhere. In detail, the chain starts and ends with ${\int_X f \, d\mu,}$ so the lower and the upper limit of ${\int_X f_n \, d\mu}$ are both equal to ${\int_X f \, d\mu,}$ and the limit exists. $\blacksquare$

**Remark 6 (Reverse monotone convergence).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space and let $(f_n)$ be a sequence of measurable functions ${f_n \colon X \to [0, +\infty).}$ Assume that ${f_{n+1} \le f_n}$ almost everywhere for every ${n.}$ Let ${f \colon X \to [0, +\infty]}$ be such that ${f = \lim_n f_n}$ almost everywhere, that is, ${f(x) = \inf_n f_n(x)}$ for almost every ${x.}$ Suppose that ${\int_X f_0 \, d\mu < +\infty.}$ Then

$$
\lim_n \int_X f_n \, d\mu = \int_X f \, d\mu, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad \inf_n \int_X f_n \, d\mu = \int_X \inf_n f_n \, d\mu.
$$

**Proof (sketch).** Let ${h_n = f_0 - f_n.}$ Then ${h_{n+1} \ge h_n}$ almost everywhere, and the monotone convergence theorem, Theorem 4, applies to ${(h_n).}$

In detail, ${h_n \to f_0 - f}$ almost everywhere and ${\int_X f_0 \, d\mu < +\infty,}$ so the conclusion of Theorem 4 for $(h_n)$ reads

$$
\int_X f_0 \, d\mu - \int_X f_n \, d\mu \longrightarrow \int_X f_0 \, d\mu - \int_X f \, d\mu. \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

**Remark 7.** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space and let $(f_n)$ be a sequence of measurable functions ${f_n \colon X \to [0, +\infty].}$ Then

$$
\int_X \Big( \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f_n \Big) \, d\mu = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \int_X f_n \, d\mu,
$$

where the series inside the integral is the function ${f \colon X \to [0, +\infty]}$ defined by

$$
f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f_n(x).
$$

**Proof (complete).** The partial sums

$$
S_N = \sum_{n=0}^{N} f_n
$$

satisfy ${S_N \ge S_{N-1}}$ for every ${N \ge 1}$ and converge to $f$ pointwise. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_X \lim_{N \to \infty} \Big( \sum_{n=0}^{N} f_n \Big) \, d\mu &= \lim_N \int_X \sum_{n=0}^{N} f_n \, d\mu \\
&= \lim_N \sum_{n=0}^{N} \int_X f_n \, d\mu \\
&= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \int_X f_n \, d\mu,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the first equality is the monotone convergence theorem, Theorem 4. Indeed, the second equality is (5) of Remark 4 of the previous post, applied finitely many times. $\blacksquare$

**Proposition 8 (Measure induced by a nonnegative function).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space, let ${\varphi \colon X \to [0, +\infty]}$ be measurable, and define ${\nu \colon \mathcal{M} \to [0, +\infty]}$ by

$$
\nu(E) := \int_E \varphi \, d\mu.
$$

Then $\nu$ is a measure on ${\mathcal{M}.}$

**Remark 9.** Let $\varphi$ and $\nu$ be as in Proposition 8. For every measurable function ${f \colon X \to [0, +\infty),}$

$$
\int_X f \, d\nu = \int_X f \varphi \, d\mu.
$$

For example, let ${X = \mathbb{R},}$ ${\mu = \lambda}$ and ${\varphi \colon \mathbb{R} \to [0, +\infty]}$ be measurable: Figure 6 draws $\lambda$ and the measure ${\nu,}$ written ${\varphi \lambda.}$

**Figure 6.** The drawing of Remark 9: on the left the Lebesgue measure ${\lambda,}$ drawn as a horizontal line, and on the right, after the arrow, the measure ${\varphi \lambda,}$ drawn as the graph of ${\varphi,}$ with the region under it over a small interval hatched.

**Proof (sketch).** Only the $\sigma$-additivity of the function $\nu$ of Proposition 8 is proved. Let ${(E_n) \subset \mathcal{M}}$ be pairwise disjoint and let ${E = \bigcup_n E_n.}$ The goal is

$$
\nu(E) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \nu(E_n), \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad \int_E \varphi \, d\mu = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \int_{E_n} \varphi \, d\mu.
$$

We compute, as in Figure 7,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \int_{E_n} \varphi \, d\mu &= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \int_X \varphi \chi_{E_n} \, d\mu \\
&= \int_X \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \varphi \chi_{E_n} \, d\mu \\
&= \int_X \varphi \chi_E \, d\mu \\
&= \int_E \varphi \, d\mu,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the second equality is Remark 7 and the third holds because the series inside the integral is ${\varphi \chi_E.}$ Indeed, each point of $E$ lies in exactly one of the pairwise disjoint sets ${E_n,}$ and no point outside $E$ lies in any of them. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 10 (Dominated convergence).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space, let $(f_n)$ be a sequence of measurable functions ${f_n \colon X \to \mathbb{R},}$ and let ${f \colon X \to \mathbb{R}.}$ Suppose that ${f_n \to f}$ almost everywhere and that there exists ${g \in \mathcal{L}^1(X)}$ such that ${|f_n| \le g}$ almost everywhere for every ${n,}$ as in Figure 8. Then ${f \in \mathcal{L}^1(X)}$ and

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_X |f_n - f| \, d\mu = 0.
$$

**Proof (complete).** At almost every ${x \in X,}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
|f(x)| &= \lim_{n \to \infty} |f_n(x)| \\
&\le g(x),
\end{aligned}
$$

because ${|f_n(x)| \le g(x)}$ for every $n$ at almost every ${x.}$ Hence

$$
\int_X |f| \, d\mu \le \int_X g \, d\mu < +\infty,
$$

and ${f \in \mathcal{L}^1.}$

(i) For every ${n,}$ almost everywhere,

$$
|f_n - f| \le |f_n| + |f| \le 2g \in \mathcal{L}^1,
$$

where ${|f_n| \le g}$ almost everywhere by assumption and ${|f| \le g}$ almost everywhere by the first step.

(ii) By Fatou's lemma, Theorem 1, and by the reverse Fatou's lemma, Remark 3, applied to the sequence $(|f_n - f|)$ with $2g$ in place of ${g,}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 &= \int_X \varliminf_n |f_n - f| \, d\mu \\
&\le \varliminf_n \int_X |f_n - f| \, d\mu \\
&\le \varlimsup_n \int_X |f_n - f| \, d\mu \\
&\le \int_X \varlimsup_n |f_n - f| \, d\mu \\
&= 0,
\end{aligned}
$$

where ${\varliminf_n |f_n - f| = \varlimsup_n |f_n - f| = 0}$ almost everywhere. Hence the limit of ${\int_X |f_n - f| \, d\mu}$ exists and is ${0.}$ $\blacksquare$

**Remark 11.** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space and let ${(f_n) \subset \mathcal{L}^1(X).}$ Suppose that

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \int_X |f_n| \, d\mu < +\infty.
$$

Then

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \int_X f_n \, d\mu = \int_X \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f_n \, d\mu.
$$

In detail, the series of the functions $f_n$ in Remark 11 converges almost everywhere. By Remark 7 the series of the functions $|f_n|$ has a finite integral, hence it is finite almost everywhere by (2) of Corollary 10 of the previous post.

## The $L^p$ spaces

Theorem 10 and Remark 11 concern the set ${\mathcal{L}^1(X)}$ of the integrable functions of Definition 11 of the previous post.

**Definition 12.** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space. For every ${p \in [1, +\infty)}$ we define

$$
\mathcal{L}^p(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu) := \Big\{ f \colon X \to \mathbb{R} : f \text{ measurable}, \ \int_X |f|^p \, d\mu < +\infty \Big\}.
$$

**Remark 13.** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space and let ${p \in [1, +\infty).}$\
**(i)** If ${f, g \colon X \to \mathbb{R}}$ are measurable and ${f = g}$ almost everywhere, then ${\int_X |f|^p \, d\mu = \int_X |g|^p \, d\mu.}$

**(ii)** For functions ${f, g \colon X \to \mathbb{R},}$ the relation given by ${f \sim g}$ if and only if ${f = g}$ almost everywhere is an equivalence relation. Hence we can consider the equivalence class of a function ${f,}$ as in Figure 9,

$$
[f] = \{ g : g \sim f \}.
$$

<Representatives />

**Figure 9.** An instance on an interval with ${\mu = \lambda{:}}$ two functions $f$ and $g$ that are equal except at one point ${x_0,}$ so that ${f \sim g}$ and ${[f] = [g].}$

**Definition 14 ($L^p$ spaces).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space and let ${p \in [1, +\infty).}$ We define

$$
L^p(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu) := \{ [f] : f \in \mathcal{L}^p(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu) \},
$$

also written ${L^p(X)}$ or ${L^p.}$

**Notation.** The elements of $L^p$ are indicated simply by ${f,}$ thinking of $f$ as a representative of ${[f].}$

**Remark 15.** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space, let ${p \in [1, +\infty)}$ and let ${f, g \in L^p.}$\
**(1)** It makes sense to write ${\int_X |f|^p \, d\mu,}$ because it does not depend on the choice of the representative. It makes sense to write ${f + g}$ and ${f \cdot g,}$ since ${f + g}$ is a representative of the class ${[f + g]}$ for any choice of representatives $f$ and $g$ of $[f]$ and ${[g],}$ and the same holds for ${f \cdot g.}$ It makes sense to write ${f \ge g}$ almost everywhere.\
**(2)** It does not make sense to write ${f(x_0)}$ for a fixed ${x_0 \in X,}$ if ${\mu(\{x_0\}) = 0.}$ It does not make sense to write ${f \ge g}$ for every ${x \in X.}$

**Remark 16.** Let ${(f_n) \subset L^p}$ and ${f \in L^p.}$ Then it makes sense to write ${f_n \to f}$ almost everywhere. We also write

$$
C([0, 1]) \subset L^p([0, 1], \mathcal{L}([0, 1]), \lambda),
$$

where ${C([0, 1])}$ is the set of the continuous functions on ${[0, 1].}$

**Definition 17 ($L^p$-norm).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space and let ${p \in [1, +\infty).}$ For ${f \in L^p(X)}$ we define the *$L^p$-norm* of ${f,}$ drawn for an instance in Figure 10, as

$$
\|f\|_p := \Big( \int_X |f|^p \, d\mu \Big)^{\frac{1}{p}}.
$$

**Notation.** The norm ${\|f\|_p}$ is also written ${\|f\|_{L^p}}$ or ${\|f\|_{L^p(X)}.}$

**Figure 10.** An instance on ${X = [0, 1]}$ with ${\mu = \lambda{:}}$ the graph of $|f|$ and, dashed, the level ${\|f\|_p}$ for the $p$ of the slider.

**Remark 18.** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space and let ${p \in [1, +\infty).}$\
**(i)** The set ${L^p(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ is a vector space.

**(ii)** If ${f, g \in L^p(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu),}$ then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|f - g\|_p = 0 &\iff f = g \text{ in } L^p \\
&\iff f = g \text{ almost everywhere}.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Proof (sketch).** In (i) only sums are checked. For ${f, g \in L^p(X),}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_X |f + g|^p \, d\mu &\le \int_X 2^{p-1} \big( |f|^p + |g|^p \big) \, d\mu \\
&= 2^{p-1} \Big( \int_X |f|^p \, d\mu + \int_X |g|^p \, d\mu \Big) \\
&< +\infty,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the inequality uses, for ${a, b \in \mathbb{R},}$

$$
|a + b|^p \le 2^{p-1} \big( |a|^p + |b|^p \big).
$$

In (ii), the equivalence of ${\|f - g\|_p = 0}$ and ${f = g}$ almost everywhere is the vanishing lemma for nonnegative functions, (1) of Corollary 10 of the previous post, applied to ${|f - g|^p.}$ $\blacksquare$

**Definition 19 (Essentially bounded functions and $L^\infty$).** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space.\
**(i)** A measurable function ${f \colon X \to \mathbb{R}}$ is said to be *essentially bounded* if there exists ${M \ge 0}$ such that ${\mu(\{ |f| > M \}) = 0,}$ as in Figure 11.

**(ii)** For a measurable function ${f \colon X \to \mathbb{R}}$ we define

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_X f &:= \inf \{ M \in \mathbb{R} : \mu(\{ f > M \}) = 0 \}, \\
\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_X f &:= \sup \{ M \in \mathbb{R} : \mu(\{ f < M \}) = 0 \},
\end{aligned}
$$

and ${\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_X f}$ is called the *essential supremum* of ${f.}$

**(iii)** We define

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}^\infty(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu) &:= \{ f \colon X \to \mathbb{R} : f \text{ measurable and essentially bounded} \}, \\
L^\infty(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu) &:= \{ [f] : f \in \mathcal{L}^\infty(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu) \}.
\end{aligned}
$$

**(iv)** For ${f \in L^\infty(X)}$ we define the *$L^\infty$-norm* ${\|f\|_\infty := \operatorname*{ess\,sup}_X |f|,}$ also written ${\|f\|_{L^\infty}}$ or ${\|f\|_{L^\infty(X)}.}$

**Remark 20.** If ${f \in L^\infty(X),}$ then ${|f| \le \|f\|_\infty}$ almost everywhere.

**Lemma 21.** Let ${(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)}$ be a measure space with ${\mu(X) < +\infty,}$ and let ${f \colon X \to \mathbb{R}}$ be measurable. Then ${f \in L^\infty(X)}$ if and only if the limit ${\lim_{p \to +\infty} \|f\|_p}$ exists and is finite. Moreover, in this case,

$$
\lim_{p \to \infty} \|f\|_p = \|f\|_\infty.
$$
