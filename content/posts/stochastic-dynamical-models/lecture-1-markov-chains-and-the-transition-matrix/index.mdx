A Markov chain is a sequence of random variables $X_0, X_1, X_2, \dots$ with values in a countable set, in which the distribution of the next value depends only on the current one. The chain is determined by the distribution of $X_0$ and by a matrix of one-step transition probabilities, and the probabilities after $n$ steps are the entries of the $n$-th power of that matrix.

[Markov chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chain) were introduced in 1906 by Andrey Andreyevich Markov (1856–1922) and are named after him.

## A frog on seven lily pads

**Example 1.1.** A frog hops among 7 lily pads, numbered from 1 to 7. The number next to each arrow of the diagram is the probability that, at the next jump, the frog moves along that arrow to a neighbouring pad. When the probabilities on the arrows leaving a pad sum to less than 1, the frog stays where it is with the remaining probability, which the diagram draws as a loop.

<FrogGraph />

The pads are the 7 *states*. The probabilities of the jumps form the matrix

$$
P = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[4pt]
0 & \frac12 & \frac12 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[4pt]
\frac12 & 0 & \frac12 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[4pt]
0 & 0 & \frac14 & \frac12 & \frac14 & 0 & 0 \\[4pt]
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \frac12 & \frac12 \\[4pt]
0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[4pt]
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix},
$$

whose entry $p_{ij}$, in row $i$ and column $j$, is the probability that the next jump takes the frog to state $j$ when it is in state $i$. For example, $p_{57} = \frac12$ is the probability that, starting in state 5, the next jump takes the frog to state 7.

We would like to know where the frog goes, how long it takes to get there, and what happens in the long run. Write $p^{(n)}_{11}$ for the probability that the frog, starting in state 1, is in state 1 after $n$ jumps. The questions below come with their answers.

**(a)** Starting in state 1, what is the probability that the frog is in state 1 after 3 jumps, after 5 jumps, after 1000 jumps? The answers are $p^{(3)}_{11} = \frac14$, $p^{(5)}_{11} = \frac{3}{16}$, and approximately $\frac15$ after 1000 jumps, since $\lim_{n \to \infty} p^{(n)}_{11} = \frac15$. Indeed, for $n = 3$ the only sequence of jumps from state 1 back to state 1 with positive probability visits the states 1, 2, 3, 1, and its probability is $1 \cdot \frac12 \cdot \frac12 = \frac14$.

**(b)** Starting in state 4, what is the probability that the frog ever reaches state 7? The answer is $\frac13$.

**(c)** Starting in state 4, what is the expected number of jumps until the frog reaches either state 3 or state 7? The answer is $\frac{11}{3}$.

**(d)** Starting in state 2, what is the long-run proportion of time that the frog spends in state 3? The answer is $\frac25$.

**Remark.** Markov chain models and methods answer questions such as the following. How long does it take to shuffle a deck of cards? How likely is a queue to overflow its buffer? A knight moves on a chessboard, choosing each move uniformly at random among its legal moves: how long does it take to return to its initial square? The expected number of moves is 168 when the knight starts in a corner and 42 when it starts on one of the sixteen central squares. What do the hyperlinks between web pages say about their relative popularity?

## Probability spaces and random variables

The following standard definitions fix the language used below.

**Definition (Probability space).** A [probability space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_space) is a triple $(\Omega, \mathscr{F}, P)$. The *sample space* $\Omega$ is the set of all possible outcomes of a random process. The *event space* $\mathscr{F}$ is a family of subsets of $\Omega$, called *events*, which contains $\Omega$ and is closed under complements and countable unions, that is, a $\sigma$-algebra on $\Omega$. The *probability* $P : \mathscr{F} \to [0, 1]$ assigns to every event a number between 0 and 1, with $P(\Omega) = 1$ and

$$
P\Big( \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} A_k \Big) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} P(A_k)
$$

for every sequence $A_1, A_2, \dots$ of pairwise disjoint events.

**Definition (Random variable).** Let $(E, \mathscr{E})$ be a measurable space, that is, a set $E$ together with a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathscr{E}$ of subsets of $E$. A [random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variable) with values in $E$ is a measurable function $X : \Omega \to E$, that is, a function such that

$$
X^{-1}(B) = \{ \omega \in \Omega : X(\omega) \in B \} \in \mathscr{F} \quad \text{for every } B \in \mathscr{E}.
$$

When $E$ is a countable set $I$ and $\mathscr{E}$ is the family of all subsets of $I$, a function $X : \Omega \to I$ is a random variable if and only if the set $\{ X = i \} = \{ \omega \in \Omega : X(\omega) = i \}$ belongs to $\mathscr{F}$ for every $i \in I$. Indeed, every subset of $I$ is a countable union of singletons, and $\mathscr{F}$ is closed under countable unions.

**Definition (Distribution).** The [probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distribution) of a random variable $X$ with values in $E$ describes how probability is assigned to the values of $X$. It is the probability $B \mapsto P(X \in B)$ on $(E, \mathscr{E})$. When $E$ is a countable set $I$, the distribution of $X$ is determined by the numbers $P(X = i)$ with $i \in I$. Indeed, $P(X \in B) = \sum_{i \in B} P(X = i)$ for every $B \subseteq I$, by countable additivity.

**Example.** A sequence of positions of the frog of Example 1.1 is the outcome of a random experiment. To observe the frog for ten jumps, take as sample space the set $\Omega = \{1, \dots, 7\}^{11}$ of all sequences $\omega = (\omega_0, \omega_1, \dots, \omega_{10})$ of eleven pads, as event space $\mathscr{F}$ the family of all subsets of $\Omega$, and as $P$ a probability on $\mathscr{F}$. For $n = 0, 1, \dots, 10$, the position of the frog after $n$ jumps is the random variable $X_n(\omega) = \omega_n$. The outcome

$$
\omega = (1, 2, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3, 3, 1, 2, 3)
$$

gives $X_0(\omega) = 1$, $X_1(\omega) = 2$, $X_2(\omega) = 2$ and $X_3(\omega) = 3$. A random variable takes as input an outcome, which is a single element of $\Omega$ and not an event, and returns a value. An event is a set of outcomes, for example $\{ X_{10} = 3 \} = \{ \omega \in \Omega : \omega_{10} = 3 \}$.

## Markov chains

Let $I$ be a countable set, with elements denoted $i, j, k, \dots$. Each $i \in I$ is called a *state* and $I$ is called the *state space*.

We work in a probability space $(\Omega, \mathscr{F}, P)$: $\Omega$ is a set of outcomes, $\mathscr{F}$ is a set of subsets of $\Omega$, and for $A \in \mathscr{F}$ the number $P(A)$ is the probability of $A$. The object of study is a sequence of random variables $X_0, X_1, \dots$ with values in $I$ whose joint distribution is determined by simple rules. A random variable $X$ with values in $I$ is a function $X : \Omega \to I$, measurable in the sense above.

A row vector $\lambda = (\lambda_i : i \in I)$ is called a *measure* if $\lambda_i \ge 0$ for all $i \in I$. If moreover $\sum_{i \in I} \lambda_i = 1$, it is called a *distribution*, or probability measure. The chain starts from an *initial distribution* $\lambda$ over $I$, that is, numbers $\lambda_i$ with $0 \le \lambda_i \le 1$ for all $i \in I$ and $\sum_{i \in I} \lambda_i = 1$. The initial distribution is the initial condition of the chain. When the chain starts in state $i$ with probability 1, the initial distribution is denoted

$$
\lambda = \delta_i = (0, \dots, 0, 1, 0, \dots),
$$

with the entry 1 in position $i$. In other words, $(\delta_i)_j = 1$ if $j = i$ and $(\delta_i)_j = 0$ if $j \neq i$.

The chain also has a *transition matrix* $P = (p_{ij} : i, j \in I)$ with $p_{ij} \ge 0$ for all $i, j \in I$. It is a [stochastic matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_matrix), meaning that $p_{ij} \ge 0$ for all $i, j \in I$ and $\sum_{j \in I} p_{ij} = 1$ for all $i \in I$. In other words, each row of $P$ is a distribution over $I$. The letter $P$ denotes both the probability on $\mathscr{F}$ and the transition matrix. The probability is always applied to an event, as in $P(A)$, while the matrix appears through its entries $p_{ij}$ or in matrix products.

**Definition 1.2.** Let $\lambda$ be a distribution over $I$ and $P$ a stochastic matrix. A sequence $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ of random variables with values in $I$ is a *Markov chain* with initial distribution $\lambda$ and transition matrix $P$ if the following two conditions hold.

**(i)** For every $i_0 \in I$, $P(X_0 = i_0) = \lambda_{i_0}$.

**(ii)** For every $n \ge 0$ and all $i_0, \dots, i_{n+1} \in I$ with $P(X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_n = i_n) > 0$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&P(X_{n+1} = i_{n+1} \mid X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_n = i_n) \\
&\qquad = P(X_{n+1} = i_{n+1} \mid X_n = i_n) = p_{i_n i_{n+1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Indeed, the condition $P(X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_n = i_n) > 0$ implies $P(X_n = i_n) > 0$, so both conditional probabilities in (ii) are defined. For short, we say that $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ is *Markov*$(\lambda, P)$.

Checking conditions (i) and (ii) is usually the most direct way to decide whether a given random process $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ is a Markov chain. The following theorem gives an equivalent description.

**Theorem 1.3.** A sequence $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ of random variables with values in $I$ is *Markov*$(\lambda, P)$ if and only if, for all $n \ge 0$ and $i_0, \dots, i_n \in I$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&P(X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_n = i_n) \\
&\quad = \lambda_{i_0} p_{i_0 i_1} \cdots p_{i_{n-1} i_n}.
\end{aligned} \tag{1.1}
$$

For $n = 0$ the right-hand side of (1.1) is $\lambda_{i_0}$.

**Proof (complete).** Suppose that $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ is *Markov*$(\lambda, P)$. For $n = 0$, (1.1) is condition (i). For $n \ge 1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&P(X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_n = i_n) \\
&\quad = P(X_n = i_n \mid X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_{n-1} = i_{n-1}) \\
&\qquad \times P(X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_{n-1} = i_{n-1}) \\
&\quad = P(X_0 = i_0) \, P(X_1 = i_1 \mid X_0 = i_0) \\
&\qquad \cdots P(X_n = i_n \mid X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_{n-1} = i_{n-1}) \\
&\quad = \lambda_{i_0} p_{i_0 i_1} \cdots p_{i_{n-1} i_n}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Indeed, the first equality is the definition of conditional probability, the second repeats the same step for the events $\{ X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_m = i_m \}$ with $m = n - 1, n - 2, \dots, 1$, and the third follows from (i) and (ii). These steps require $P(X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_{n-1} = i_{n-1}) > 0$, which makes every conditioning event have positive probability.

Indeed, when $P(X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_{n-1} = i_{n-1}) = 0$, both sides of (1.1) are zero. The left-hand side is at most this probability. For the right-hand side, let $m$ be the smallest index with $P(X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_m = i_m) = 0$. If $m = 0$, then $\lambda_{i_0} = 0$ by (i). If $m \ge 1$, the first equality of the computation above and condition (ii), with $m$ in place of $n$, give $0 = p_{i_{m-1} i_m} P(X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_{m-1} = i_{m-1})$ with a positive second factor, so $p_{i_{m-1} i_m} = 0$.

On the other hand, suppose that (1.1) holds for all $n \ge 0$ and $i_0, \dots, i_n \in I$. Summing (1.1) over all $i_1, \dots, i_n \in I$ gives $P(X_0 = i_0) = \lambda_{i_0}$, that is, (i). Indeed, the events $\{ X_0 = i_0, X_1 = i_1, \dots, X_n = i_n \}$ with $(i_1, \dots, i_n) \in I^n$ form a countable family of pairwise disjoint events whose union is $\{ X_0 = i_0 \}$. On the right-hand side, the sum of $p_{i_{n-1} i_n}$ over $i_n$ is 1, then the sum of $p_{i_{n-2} i_{n-1}}$ over $i_{n-1}$ is 1, and so on down to $i_1$.

Then, for $n \ge 1$, summing (1.1) over $i_n$ gives $P(X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_{n-1} = i_{n-1}) = \lambda_{i_0} p_{i_0 i_1} \cdots p_{i_{n-2} i_{n-1}}$. Hence, whenever this probability is positive,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&P(X_n = i_n \mid X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_{n-1} = i_{n-1}) \\
&\qquad = \frac{P(X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_n = i_n)}{P(X_0 = i_0, \dots, X_{n-1} = i_{n-1})} = p_{i_{n-1} i_n},
\end{aligned}
$$

which establishes (ii), with $n - 1$ in place of $n$. Indeed, the middle term of (ii) also equals $p_{i_{n-1} i_n}$. For $n = 1$ it coincides with the first term. For $n \ge 2$, summing (1.1) over $i_0, \dots, i_{n-2}$ gives $P(X_{n-1} = i_{n-1}, X_n = i_n) = P(X_{n-1} = i_{n-1}) \, p_{i_{n-1} i_n}$, because the same sum of (1.1) at time $n - 1$ equals $P(X_{n-1} = i_{n-1})$, which is positive. $\blacksquare$

## Construction from independent random variables

At each time, a new source of randomness determines the next step, through a rule that depends only on the current state. Let $U_1, U_2, \dots$ be [independent and identically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_and_identically_distributed_random_variables) random variables with values in a measurable space $(E, \mathscr{E})$: every $U_n$ has the same distribution, and the $U_n$ are mutually independent. Let $F : I \times E \to I$ be a function such that $u \mapsto F(j, u)$ is measurable for every $j \in I$.

Take $i \in I$. Set $X_0 = i$ and define recursively

$$
X_{n+1} = F(X_n, U_{n+1}), \qquad n \ge 0.
$$

Then $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ is *Markov*$(\delta_i, P)$, where $p_{jk} = P(F(j, U) = k)$ for all $j, k \in I$ and $U$ is a random variable with the common distribution of the $U_n$.

Indeed, $P$ is a stochastic matrix, because the events $\{ F(j, U) = k \}$ with $k \in I$ are pairwise disjoint with union $\Omega$. The variables $X_0, \dots, X_n$ are functions of $U_1, \dots, U_n$, so $U_{n+1}$ is independent of them, and on the event $\{ X_n = j \}$ we have $X_{n+1} = F(j, U_{n+1})$. Hence both conditional probabilities in condition (ii) equal $P(F(j, U_{n+1}) = k) = p_{jk}$, while condition (i) holds with $\lambda = \delta_i$ because $X_0 = i$.

$$

U &\in [0, p_{i1}) & &\Longrightarrow \quad J = 1, \\
U &\in \Big[ \sum_{k=1}^{j-1} p_{ik}, \ \sum_{k=1}^{j} p_{ik} \Big), \quad j \ge 2 & &\Longrightarrow \quad J = j.

$$

Indeed, for fixed $i$ these intervals are pairwise disjoint, the interval of $J = j$ has length $p_{ij}$, and their union is $[0, 1)$ because $\sum_{j} p_{ij} = 1$. The value $U = 1$ has probability zero and may be assigned to any state. The map $u \mapsto F(i, u)$ is measurable, since it is constant on each interval. Hence $P(F(i, U) = j) = p_{ij}$ for all $i, j \in I$, and the construction of the previous section, with $E = [0, 1]$, produces a chain that is *Markov*$(\delta_i, P)$.

## The $n$-step transition matrix

Let $A$ be an event. A convenient notation is

$$
P_i(A) = P(A \mid X_0 = i),
$$

for every state $i$ with $\lambda_i = P(X_0 = i) > 0$. For example, $P_i(X_1 = j) = p_{ij}$ by condition (ii) with $n = 0$.

Given the initial distribution $\lambda$, we treat it as a row vector. Then

$$
P(X_1 = j) = \sum_{i \in I} \lambda_i P_i(X_1 = j) = \sum_{i \in I} \lambda_i p_{ij}.
$$

Here and below, a term $\lambda_i P_i(\cdot)$ with $\lambda_i = 0$ is read as 0, because $P_i$ is defined only when $\lambda_i > 0$. Indeed, the events $\{ X_0 = i \}$ with $i \in I$ are pairwise disjoint with union $\Omega$, and $P(X_0 = i, X_1 = j) = \lambda_i p_{ij}$ by (1.1). This equals $\lambda_i P_i(X_1 = j)$ when $\lambda_i > 0$ and is zero when $\lambda_i = 0$. Similarly,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_i(X_2 = j) &= \sum_{k \in I} P_i(X_1 = k, X_2 = j) \\
&= \sum_{k \in I} p_{ik} p_{kj} = (P^2)_{ij}, \\
P(X_2 = j) &= \sum_{i, k \in I} \lambda_i P_i(X_1 = k, X_2 = j) \\
&= \sum_{i, k \in I} \lambda_i p_{ik} p_{kj} = (\lambda P^2)_j.
\end{aligned}
$$

Indeed, (1.1) gives $P(X_0 = i, X_1 = k, X_2 = j) = \lambda_i p_{ik} p_{kj}$, and dividing by $\lambda_i$ gives $P_i(X_1 = k, X_2 = j) = p_{ik} p_{kj}$.

Write $p^{(n)}_{ij}$ for the entry $(i, j)$ of the matrix $P^n$, which depends on $P$ and not on $\lambda$. Continuing in this way,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_i(X_n = j) &= (\delta_i P^n)_j = (P^n)_{ij} = p^{(n)}_{ij}, \\
P(X_n = j) &= \sum_{i_0, \dots, i_{n-1} \in I} \lambda_{i_0} p_{i_0 i_1} \cdots p_{i_{n-1} j} \\
&= (\lambda P^n)_j,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the first line holds for every state $i$ with $\lambda_i > 0$. Indeed, the second line sums (1.1), with $i_n = j$, over $i_0, \dots, i_{n-1}$, and this sum is the entry $j$ of the row vector $\lambda P^n$. For the first line with $n = 0$, $P_i(X_0 = j) = (\delta_i)_j$. For $n \ge 1$, summing (1.1) with $i_0 = i$ and $i_n = j$ over $i_1, \dots, i_{n-1}$ and dividing by $\lambda_i$ gives $P_i(X_n = j) = \sum_{i_1, \dots, i_{n-1} \in I} p_{i i_1} \cdots p_{i_{n-1} j} = (P^n)_{ij}$. The right-hand side $(\delta_i P^n)_j$ of the first line is the right-hand side $(\lambda P^n)_j$ of the second one for the initial distribution $\lambda = \delta_i$, a special case of $\lambda$.

When $I$ is infinite, these matrix products are series of nonnegative terms, and they converge. Indeed, by induction on $n$, every row of $P^n$ has nonnegative entries with sum 1, since $\sum_{j} (P^{n+1})_{ij} = \sum_{k} (P^n)_{ik} \sum_{j} p_{kj} = \sum_{k} (P^n)_{ik} = 1$, where the order of summation can be exchanged because all terms are nonnegative. So every entry of $P^n$ lies in $[0, 1]$, and every series above has partial sums bounded by 1.

Thus $P^{(n)} = (p^{(n)}_{ij} : i, j \in I)$, the *$n$-step transition matrix*, is simply $P^n$, the matrix $P$ raised to the power $n$, and $p^{(n)}_{ij} = P_i(X_n = j)$ is the probability of moving from state $i$ to state $j$ in $n$ steps whenever $\lambda_i > 0$. For $n = 0$, $P^0$ is the identity matrix, so $p^{(0)}_{ij}$ is 1 if $j = i$ and 0 otherwise.

Also, for all $i, j \in I$ and $n, m \ge 0$, the *Chapman–Kolmogorov equations* hold:

$$
p^{(n+m)}_{ij} = \sum_{k \in I} p^{(n)}_{ik} p^{(m)}_{kj}.
$$

Indeed, the right-hand side is the entry $(i, j)$ of $P^n P^m = P^{n+m}$. The [Chapman–Kolmogorov equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapman%E2%80%93Kolmogorov_equation) are named after Sydney Chapman (1888–1970) and Andrey Kolmogorov (1903–1987), who formulated them independently.

In Example 1.1, as $n \to \infty$ the matrix $P^{(n)} = P^n$ converges entrywise:

$$
P^{(n)} \to \begin{pmatrix}
\frac15 & \frac25 & \frac25 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[6pt]
\frac15 & \frac25 & \frac25 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[6pt]
\frac15 & \frac25 & \frac25 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[6pt]
\frac{2}{15} & \frac{4}{15} & \frac{4}{15} & 0 & 0 & 0 & \frac13 \\[6pt]
\frac{1}{15} & \frac{2}{15} & \frac{2}{15} & 0 & 0 & 0 & \frac23 \\[6pt]
\frac{2}{15} & \frac{4}{15} & \frac{4}{15} & 0 & 0 & 0 & \frac13 \\[6pt]
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

## $P^{(n)}$ for a two-state Markov chain

**Example 1.4 (A two-state Markov chain).** Let $\alpha, \beta \in [0, 1]$ with $\alpha + \beta > 0$, and consider the chain on $I = \{1, 2\}$ with transition matrix

$$
P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - \alpha & \alpha \\ \beta & 1 - \beta \end{pmatrix}.
$$

From state 1 the chain moves to state 2 with probability $\alpha$, and from state 2 it moves to state 1 with probability $\beta$. The hypothesis $\alpha + \beta > 0$ excludes only the identity matrix, and it is needed because the formulas below divide by $\alpha + \beta$.

The eigenvalues of $P$ are $1$ and $1 - \alpha - \beta$. Indeed, $\det(x \, \mathrm{Id} - P) = (x - 1)(x - 1 + \alpha + \beta)$, where $\mathrm{Id}$ is the $2 \times 2$ identity matrix. So we can write

$$
\begin{aligned}
&P = U \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 - \alpha - \beta \end{pmatrix} U^{-1} \\[4pt]
&\quad \Longrightarrow \quad P^n = U \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & (1 - \alpha - \beta)^n \end{pmatrix} U^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

for an invertible $2 \times 2$ matrix $U$. Indeed, the two eigenvalues are distinct because $\alpha + \beta > 0$, so $P$ is diagonalizable, and in the product of $n$ copies of $P$ the inner factors $U^{-1} U$ cancel.

So $p^{(n)}_{11} = A + B (1 - \alpha - \beta)^n$ for some constants $A$ and $B$ that do not depend on $n$, with the convention $0^0 = 1$. Indeed, each entry of $P^n$ is a linear combination of $1$ and $(1 - \alpha - \beta)^n$ with coefficients taken from $U$ and $U^{-1}$.

But $p^{(0)}_{11} = 1 = A + B$ and $p^{(1)}_{11} = 1 - \alpha = A + B (1 - \alpha - \beta)$. So $(A, B) = (\beta, \alpha)/(\alpha + \beta)$, that is, for a chain with $\lambda_1 > 0$, for instance with $\lambda = \delta_1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_1(X_n = 1) = p^{(n)}_{11} &= \frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta} \\
&\quad + \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} (1 - \alpha - \beta)^n.
\end{aligned}
$$

Indeed, subtracting the two equations gives $B (\alpha + \beta) = \alpha$, and then $A = 1 - B$.

When $\alpha + \beta < 2$, this tends exponentially fast to the limit $\beta/(\alpha + \beta)$. Indeed, then $|1 - \alpha - \beta| < 1$ and

$$
\Big| p^{(n)}_{11} - \frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta} \Big| = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} \, |1 - \alpha - \beta|^n.
$$

For $\alpha = \beta = 1$ the formula gives $p^{(n)}_{11} = \frac{1 + (-1)^n}{2}$, which alternates between 1 and 0 and has no limit.

The other entries of $P^n$ are computed in the same way, and

$$
P^n = \begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta} + \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} (1 - \alpha - \beta)^n & \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} - \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} (1 - \alpha - \beta)^n \\[6pt]
\frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta} - \frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta} (1 - \alpha - \beta)^n & \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} + \frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta} (1 - \alpha - \beta)^n
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Indeed, $p^{(n)}_{12} = 1 - p^{(n)}_{11}$ because the rows of $P^n$ sum to 1. Exchanging the two states, which exchanges $\alpha$ and $\beta$, gives $p^{(n)}_{22}$, and then $p^{(n)}_{21} = 1 - p^{(n)}_{22}$.

The figure draws the chain for the values of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ set by the sliders, and plots $p^{(n)}_{11}$ for $n = 0, 1, \dots, 30$ together with the level $\beta/(\alpha + \beta)$ when $\alpha + \beta > 0$. When $\alpha + \beta > 1$ the factor $1 - \alpha - \beta$ is negative, and the values alternate above and below the level.

**Remark.** The same answer can be reached through a recurrence. For $n \ge 1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
p^{(n)}_{11} &= p^{(n-1)}_{11} p_{11} + p^{(n-1)}_{12} p_{21} \\
&= p^{(n-1)}_{11} (1 - \alpha) + \big( 1 - p^{(n-1)}_{11} \big) \beta.
\end{aligned}
$$

Indeed, the first equality is the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation with $n - 1$ and $1$ in place of $n$ and $m$, and the second uses $p^{(n-1)}_{12} = 1 - p^{(n-1)}_{11}$. This gives the linear [recurrence relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recurrence_relation)

$$
p^{(n)}_{11} = \beta + (1 - \alpha - \beta) \, p^{(n-1)}_{11},
$$

an equation that expresses each term of the sequence $\big( p^{(n)}_{11} \big)_{n \ge 0}$ after the first as a function of the preceding one. Its solution has the form $p^{(n)}_{11} = A + B (1 - \alpha - \beta)^n$. Indeed, the constant $\frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta}$ satisfies the recurrence, so the difference $d_n = p^{(n)}_{11} - \frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta}$ satisfies $d_n = (1 - \alpha - \beta) \, d_{n-1}$ and therefore $d_n = (1 - \alpha - \beta)^n d_0$.
