Which distributions of the initial state of a Markov chain are passed on unchanged to the state at every later time?

## Invariant densities

Let $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ be a Markov chain on the probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ with set of states $I$ and transition matrix ${P = (p_{ij})_{i, j \in I}.}$ As in the previous posts, $\mathbb{P}_i$ is the conditional probability given ${X_0 = i,}$ and ${p^{(n)}_{ij} = \mathbb{P}_i\{X_n = j\}}$ is the entry $(i, j)$ of the power ${P^n.}$

**Definition 5.1 (Invariant probability measure).** A probability measure $\mu$ on $I$ is called *invariant* for the Markov chain $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ if, when $X_0$ has distribution ${\mu,}$ the random variable $X_n$ has distribution $\mu$ for all ${n \ge 0.}$

In Definition 5.1 and below, we identify a probability measure on $I$ with its density with respect to the [counting measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counting_measure) on the $\sigma$-algebra of all subsets of ${I.}$ This density is the collection of numbers $(\mu_i)_{i \in I}$ in the interval $[0, 1]$ such that ${\sum_{i \in I} \mu_i = 1.}$

**Proposition 5.2.** A probability measure $\mu$ on $I$ is invariant for the Markov chain $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ if and only if, for every state ${i,}$ we have

$$
\mu_i = \sum_{j \in I} \mu_j p_{ji}, \tag{9}
$$

that is, the density of $\mu$ is a left eigenvector of the transition matrix with eigenvalue 1.

**Proof (complete).** Let $\mu$ be the probability distribution of ${X_0.}$ The probability distribution $\nu$ of $X_1$ has density

$$
\nu_i = \mathbb{P}\{X_1 = i\} = \sum_{j \in I} \mathbb{P}\{X_0 = j\} p_{ji} = \sum_{j \in I} \mu_j p_{ji}.
$$

Therefore, if $\mu$ is an invariant probability measure for the Markov chain ${(X_n)_{n \ge 0},}$ then ${\nu = \mu}$ and (9) holds.

Conversely, if $\mu$ is a probability measure on $I$ satisfying ${(9),}$ then $X_n$ has probability distribution $\mu$ for all ${n.}$ $\blacksquare$

Summing up, a probability measure $\mu$ on ${I,}$ with its density identified with the row vector ${(\mu_i)_{i \in I},}$ is invariant if and only if

$$
\mu = \mu P.
$$

**Theorem 5.3.** If the set of states $I$ is finite, then there exists at least one invariant probability measure.

**Proof (complete).** The set of probability densities on $I$

$$
\mathcal{K} = \left\{ (\mu_i)_{i \in I} : 0 \le \mu_i \le 1 \text{ for every } i, \ \sum_{i \in I} \mu_i = 1 \right\}
$$

is compact, as a closed bounded subset of ${\mathbb{R}^{\operatorname{card}(I)}.}$ Let ${\mu^{(0)} \in \mathcal{K}.}$ For all ${n \ge 1,}$ the family of real numbers $(\mu^{(n)}_j)_{j \in I}$ defined by

$$
\mu^{(n)}_j = \frac1n \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left( \sum_{i \in I} \mu^{(0)}_i p^{(k)}_{ij} \right)
$$

is a probability density on ${I.}$ Since the set $\mathcal{K}$ is compact, by considering a subsequence, if necessary, we can assume that the sequence $(\mu^{(n)}_j)_{n \ge 1}$ is convergent for all ${j \in I.}$ Defining ${\pi_j = \lim_{n \to \infty} \mu^{(n)}_j,}$ the collection of real numbers $(\pi_j)_{j \in I}$ is a probability density on $I$ and, moreover,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i \in I} \pi_i p_{ij} &= \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac1n \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left( \sum_{i \in I} \mu^{(0)}_i p^{(k+1)}_{ij} \right) \\
&= \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n+1} \sum_{k=1}^{n+1} \left( \sum_{i \in I} \mu^{(0)}_i p^{(k)}_{ij} \right) - \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n+1} \sum_{i \in I} \mu^{(0)}_i p_{ij} \\
&= \pi_j
\end{aligned}
$$

for all ${j \in I.}$ This proves that the probability measure with density $(\pi_i)_{i \in I}$ is invariant for the Markov chain. $\blacksquare$

In the proof above, the invariant probability density is obtained as a limit of subsequences of ${(\mu^{(n)})_{n \ge 1},}$ where $\mu^{(0)}$ is a density on $I$ and

$$
\mu^{(n)}_j = \frac1n \sum_{k=1}^{n} (\mu^{(0)} P^k)_j
$$

for all ${n \ge 1}$ and all ${j \in I,}$ where ${(\mu^{(0)} P^k)_j = \sum_{i \in I} \mu^{(0)}_i p^{(k)}_{ij}.}$ In particular, if ${\mu^{(0)} = \delta_i,}$ the Dirac density in ${i,}$ we have

$$
\mu^{(n)}_j = \frac1n \sum_{k=1}^{n} p^{(k)}_{ij}.
$$

The analysis of this formula allows us to interpret the meaning of an invariant distribution. Recall that the random variable

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n} \mathbf{1}_{\{X_k = j\}}
$$

represents the number of visits to $j$ up to time ${n.}$ Hence the random variable

$$
\frac1n \sum_{k=1}^{n} \mathbf{1}_{\{X_k = j\}}
$$

represents the random *frequency* of visits to the state $j$ at time ${n.}$ Thus its expectation with respect to the probability ${\mathbb{P}_i,}$

$$
\mathbb{E}_i\left[ \frac1n \sum_{k=1}^{n} \mathbf{1}_{\{X_k = j\}} \right] = \frac1n \sum_{k=1}^{n} \mathbb{P}_i\{X_k = j\} = \frac1n \sum_{k=1}^{n} p^{(k)}_{ij},
$$

represents the *average frequency* of visits to the state $j$ at time ${n.}$ Therefore we can interpret the values $\pi_j$ as *asymptotic average frequencies* of visits to the state ${j.}$

A Markov chain, even when $I$ is finite, may have more than one invariant density, as the chain of the gambler's ruin problem with states ${\{0, 1, \dots, N\}}$ shows. In this case the densities $(1, 0, \dots, 0)$ and ${(0, \dots, 0, 1),}$ and all their convex combinations

$$
\lambda (1, 0, \dots, 0) + (1 - \lambda)(0, \dots, 0, 1) = (\lambda, 0, \dots, 0, 1 - \lambda), \qquad \lambda \in [0, 1],
$$

are invariant.

### The random walk on $\mathbb{Z}$

In the case where the set $I$ is infinite, however, a Markov chain may not admit any invariant density, as Example 5.1 shows. The random walk on $\mathbb{Z}$ of the previous post, with ${p, q \in (0, 1)}$ and ${p + q = 1,}$ jumps from each state $i$ to ${i + 1}$ with probability $p$ and to ${i - 1}$ with probability ${q.}$

**Example 5.1.** The random walk on $\mathbb{Z}$ has no invariant density.

In Example 5.1, an invariant density $(\pi_i)_{i \in \mathbb{Z}}$ would satisfy ${\pi_i = q \pi_{i+1} + p \pi_{i-1}}$ for all ${i \in \mathbb{Z},}$ namely

$$
q (\pi_{i+1} - \pi_i) = p (\pi_i - \pi_{i-1}). \tag{10}
$$

We begin by observing that ${\pi_{i+1} \neq \pi_i}$ for all ${i \in \mathbb{Z}.}$ Otherwise the identity (10) implies ${\pi_{i+1} = \pi_i}$ for all ${i,}$ and $(\pi_i)_{i \in \mathbb{Z}}$ cannot be a probability density.

We distinguish three cases: ${p > q,}$ ${p = q = 1/2}$ and ${p < q.}$ In the first case, from (10) we find

$$
\pi_{i+1} - \pi_i = (p/q)^i (\pi_1 - \pi_0),
$$

from which ${\lim_{i \to +\infty} |\pi_{i+1} - \pi_i| = +\infty,}$ contradicting the boundedness of ${(\pi_i)_{i \in \mathbb{Z}}.}$ In the second case, from (10) we get the formula

$$
\pi_{i+1} - \pi_i = \pi_1 - \pi_0,
$$

from which ${\pi_i = i (\pi_1 - \pi_0) + c,}$ with $c$ constant, which again contradicts the boundedness of ${(\pi_i)_{i \in \mathbb{Z}}.}$ The third case is similar to the first one. Summing up, the random walk on $\mathbb{Z}$ has no invariant density.

### A chain on $\mathbb{N}$ with an explicit invariant density

The explicit computation of an invariant density, or just the proof of its existence, might not be simple. The Markov chain of Example 5.2 arises in several models. Among them are the gambler's ruin problem when the gambler's opponent, for example a bank, has an infinite fortune, a random walk on ${\mathbb{N},}$ and the number of customers in a queue at a counter. It is a case in which one can compute explicitly the unique invariant density.

**Example 5.2.** Consider the Markov chain with set of states ${\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}}$ and transition matrix

$$
P = \begin{pmatrix}
r_0 & p_0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots \\
q_1 & r_1 & p_1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots \\
0 & q_2 & r_2 & p_2 & 0 & \cdots \\
0 & 0 & q_3 & r_3 & p_3 & \cdots \\
0 & 0 & 0 & q_4 & r_4 & \cdots \\
\cdots & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots
\end{pmatrix},
$$

where ${(p_m)_{m \ge 0},}$ $(r_m)_{m \ge 0}$ and $(q_m)_{m > 0}$ are 3 sequences of real numbers. They satisfy ${p_m, q_m \in (0, 1)}$ and ${r_m \in [0, 1],}$ and

$$
r_0 + p_0 = 1, \qquad q_m + r_m + p_m = 1 \quad \text{for all } m \ge 1.
$$

In Example 5.2, every invariant density $(\pi_n)_{n \ge 0}$ satisfies

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pi_0 &= r_0 \pi_0 + q_1 \pi_1, \\
\pi_n &= p_{n-1} \pi_{n-1} + r_n \pi_n + q_{n+1} \pi_{n+1}
\end{aligned}
$$

for all ${n \ge 1,}$ besides positivity ${\pi_n \ge 0}$ and normalization ${\sum_{n \ge 0} \pi_n = 1.}$ From the first equation we find

$$
\pi_1 = \frac{1 - r_0}{q_1} \pi_0 = \frac{p_0}{q_1} \pi_0
$$

and, inductively,

$$
\pi_n = \frac{p_0 \cdots p_{n-1}}{q_1 \cdots q_n} \pi_0
$$

for all ${n \ge 1.}$ To see this, note that ${1 - r_n = p_n + q_n}$ for ${n \ge 1,}$ so that the equation for $\pi_n$ becomes

$$
q_{n+1} \pi_{n+1} - p_n \pi_n = q_n \pi_n - p_{n-1} \pi_{n-1}.
$$

The first equation says ${q_1 \pi_1 - p_0 \pi_0 = 0,}$ so by induction ${q_{n+1} \pi_{n+1} = p_n \pi_n}$ for every ${n \ge 0,}$ and these relations give the formula. The normalization condition is fulfilled if we set

$$
\pi_0 = \left( 1 + \sum_{n \ge 1} \frac{p_0 \cdots p_{n-1}}{q_1 \cdots q_n} \right)^{-1}
$$

if and only if the series

$$
\sum_{n \ge 1} \frac{p_0 \cdots p_{n-1}}{q_1 \cdots q_n}
$$

is convergent. Therefore this condition is necessary and sufficient for the existence of an invariant density, which is necessarily unique.
