The first entry ended with a fixed point $\pi$. The natural next claim is that $\mu_n \to \pi$ for every starting distribution. It is false, and the counterexample fits in one matrix.

$$
P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The chain swaps states at every step. The stationary distribution exists and is $\pi = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$. Start from $\mu_0 = (1, 0)$:

$$
\mu_1 = (0, 1), \quad \mu_2 = (1, 0), \quad \mu_3 = (0, 1), \ \dots
$$

The distribution never settles. It alternates forever, and its time average is $\pi$ even though the sequence has no limit.

<Flip client:visible={{ rootMargin: '600px' }} />

The linear-algebra view explains why. The eigenvalues of $P$ are $1$ and $-1$. Convergence of $\mu_0 P^n$ needs every eigenvalue other than $1$ to have modulus strictly less than one, so that its contribution decays. An eigenvalue at $-1$ does not decay; it flips sign.

The chain-level name for this is periodicity. State $1$ can only return to itself in an even number of steps, so its period is $2$. The theorem the course is heading toward says: irreducible plus aperiodic gives convergence to the unique stationary distribution from every start. Irreducible alone gives only the time average.

Exercise I set myself: take $P_\varepsilon$ with entries $1 - \varepsilon$ off the diagonal and $\varepsilon$ on it. The second eigenvalue is $1 - 2\varepsilon$, strictly inside the unit circle for any $\varepsilon > 0$, and the alternation turns into a damped oscillation. The chain forgets its start at rate $|1 - 2\varepsilon|^n$.
