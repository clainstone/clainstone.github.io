The usual Markov property splits the path of a Markov chain into past, present and future at a constant time. What happens when the split is made at a random time, such as the first entrance of the chain in a state?

## The strong Markov property

Let $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ be a Markov chain on the probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ with set of states $I$ and transition matrix ${(p_{ij})_{i, j \in I}.}$ For every $n$ in ${\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}}$ denote by $\mathcal{F}_n$ the smallest sub-$\sigma$-algebra of $\mathcal{F}$ containing the events

$$
\{ X_n = i_n, \dots, X_0 = i_0 \}, \qquad i_n, \dots, i_0 \in I.
$$

The $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}_n$ is made of the events for which one can say whether they occurred or not after observing the random variables ${X_0, \dots, X_n.}$ Indeed, for fixed $n$ the events ${\{X_n = i_n, \dots, X_0 = i_0\}}$ form a countable partition of ${\Omega,}$ since $I$ is countable, and the elements of the $\sigma$-algebra generated by a countable partition are the unions of its blocks. Roughly speaking, $\mathcal{F}_n$ represents the history of the Markov chain up to time ${n.}$ Figure 1 draws the events that generate $\mathcal{F}_n$ for an instance.

**Figure 1.** An instance with two states, ${I = \{1, 2\}.}$ Each branch from the row of $X_0$ down to the row of $X_n$ is one of the events ${\{X_n = i_n, \dots, X_0 = i_0\}}$ that generate ${\mathcal{F}_n,}$ for $n$ from 0 to 3, and the coloured branch is the event ${\{X_3 = 1, X_2 = 2, X_1 = 2, X_0 = 1\}.}$

**Definition 6.1 (Natural filtration).** The family $(\mathcal{F}_n)_{n \ge 0}$ of sub-$\sigma$-algebras of $\mathcal{F}$ is called the *natural filtration* of the Markov chain ${(X_n)_{n \ge 0}.}$

**Definition 6.2 (Stopping time).** A random variable ${T \colon \Omega \to \mathbb{N} \cup \{+\infty\}}$ is a *stopping time*, with respect to the filtration ${(\mathcal{F}_n)_{n \ge 0},}$ if the event $\{T \le n\}$ belongs to the $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}_n$ for all ${n \ge 0.}$

In the sense of Definition 6.2, for every state $j$ the time $T_j$ of the first entrance in ${j,}$ as in Definition 3.2,

$$
T_j = \inf\{ n \ge 1 : X_n = j \} \qquad (\text{with } \inf \emptyset := +\infty),
$$

is a stopping time with respect to the natural filtration $(\mathcal{F}_n)_{n \ge 0}$ because, for all ${n \ge 1,}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\{T_j \le 0\} &= \emptyset, \\
\{T_j \le n\} &= \bigcup_{\nu=1}^{n} \{X_\nu = j\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

In a similar way, for every state $j$ the time $U_j$ of the first exit from ${j,}$ defined for every ${\omega \in \Omega}$ by

$$
U_j(\omega) = \begin{cases} \min\{ n \ge 1 : X_n(\omega) \neq j \} & \text{if } X_n(\omega) \neq j \text{ for some } n \ge 1, \\ +\infty & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}
$$

is a stopping time with respect to the natural filtration $(\mathcal{F}_n)_{n \ge 0}$ because, for all ${n \ge 1,}$ we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\{U_j \le 0\} &= \emptyset, \\
\{U_j \le n\} &= \bigcup_{\nu=1}^{n} \{X_\nu \neq j\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Indeed, for ${\nu \le n}$ each event $\{X_\nu = j\}$ or $\{X_\nu \neq j\}$ is a union of the events that generate ${\mathcal{F}_n.}$ Figure 2 reads the events $\{T_j \le n\}$ and $\{U_j \le n\}$ off the states ${X_0, \dots, X_n}$ of a path.

### The first exit from a state

The distribution of the random variable $U_j$ can be found explicitly, and it gives an insight into the behaviour of a Markov chain. As in the previous posts, $\mathbb{P}_j$ is the conditional probability given ${\{X_0 = j\}.}$

**Theorem 6.3.** If ${0 < p_{jj} < 1,}$ then under $\mathbb{P}_j$ the random variable $U_j$ has [geometric distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_distribution) with parameter ${1 - p_{jj}.}$ The random variable $X_{U_j}$ defined by

$$
X_{U_j}(\omega) = \sum_{n \ge 1} X_n(\omega) \mathbf{1}_{\{U_j = n\}}(\omega)
$$

if ${U_j(\omega) < +\infty,}$ and in an arbitrary way on the set $\{U_j = +\infty\}$ (which has probability 0), satisfies, for all ${k \neq j,}$

$$
\mathbb{P}_j\{ X_{U_j} = k \} = \frac{p_{jk}}{1 - p_{jj}}.
$$

**Proof (complete).** The distribution of $U_j$ is found by noting that ${\mathbb{P}_j\{U_j > n\} = \mathbb{P}_j\{X_n = j, \dots, X_1 = j\} = (p_{jj})^n}$ for all ${n \ge 1,}$ so that

$$
\mathbb{P}_j\{ U_j = n \} = \mathbb{P}_j\{ U_j > n - 1 \} - \mathbb{P}_j\{ U_j > n \} = (1 - p_{jj}) (p_{jj})^{n-1}.
$$

The distribution of $X_{U_j}$ is found in a similar way, computing, for ${k \neq j,}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P}_j\{ X_{U_j} = k \} &= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}_j\{ X_n = k, X_{n-1} = j, \dots, X_1 = j \} \\
&= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} p_{jk} (p_{jj})^{n-1} = \frac{p_{jk}}{1 - p_{jj}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Indeed, the probability of $\{U_j > n\}$ under $\mathbb{P}_j$ is $(p_{jj})^n$ by (1.1) of the first post, also for ${n = 0}$ because ${U_j \ge 1.}$ The event ${\{X_{U_j} = k, U_j = n\}}$ is ${\{X_n = k, X_{n-1} = j, \dots, X_1 = j\},}$ of probability ${p_{jk} (p_{jj})^{n-1},}$ and $U_j$ is finite with probability ${1 - \lim_{n} (p_{jj})^n = 1.}$ $\blacksquare$

The Markov property implies that the distribution of $U_j$ is [memoryless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memorylessness), and so it must be a geometric distribution. Figures 3 and 4 draw the two distributions of Theorem 6.3 for an instance.

### The chain restarted at a stopping time

Roughly speaking, the *strong Markov property* is the Markov property when one considers past, present and future with respect to a stopping time, not a constant time as in the usual Markov property. As a first step we prove Theorem 6.4.

**Theorem 6.4.** Let $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ be a Markov chain on a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ with transition matrix $(p_{ij})_{i, j \in I}$ and let $T$ be a stopping time with respect to its natural filtration such that ${\mathbb{P}\{T < \infty\} = 1.}$ For all ${\omega \in \Omega}$ and ${n \ge 0}$ denote

$$
Y_n(\omega) = \begin{cases} X_{T(\omega)+n}(\omega) & \text{if } T(\omega) < +\infty, \\ i_\infty & \text{if } T(\omega) = +\infty, \end{cases}
$$

where $i_\infty$ is an arbitrary state. The collection of random variables $(Y_n)_{n \ge 0}$ is a Markov chain with the same transition matrix ${(p_{ij})_{i, j \in I}.}$

**Proof (complete).** First note that $Y_n$ is a random variable because, for all ${n \ge 0}$ and ${j \in I,}$ the set $\{Y_n = j\}$ is an event, that is, it belongs to ${\mathcal{F},}$ as it can be written in the form

$$
\{X_{T+n} = j\} = \bigcup_{0 \le \nu < \infty} \big( \{T = \nu\} \cap \{X_{\nu+n} = j\} \big)
$$

if ${j \neq i_\infty}$ and

$$
\{T = +\infty\} \cup \bigg( \bigcup_{0 \le \nu < \infty} \big( \{T = \nu\} \cap \{X_{\nu+n} = i_\infty\} \big) \bigg)
$$

if ${j = i_\infty.}$ Here and below, an event written with $X_T, X_{T+1}, \dots$ is contained in ${\{T < +\infty\}{:}}$ for instance, $\{X_T = i\}$ stands for ${\{T < +\infty, X_T = i\}.}$

To prove the theorem it suffices to check that, for all ${n \ge 0}$ and all finite families $(i_k)_{0 \le k \le n}$ of states such that the event ${\{Y_n = i_n, \dots, Y_0 = i_0\}}$ has strictly positive probability, and for all states ${j,}$ we have

$$
\mathbb{P}\Big( \{Y_{n+1} = j\} \;\Big|\; \bigcap_{0 \le k \le n} \{Y_k = i_k\} \Big) = \mathbb{P}\{Y_{n+1} = j \mid Y_n = i_n\} = p_{i_n j}.
$$

As a first step we show that

$$
\mathbb{P}\{X_{T+n+1} = j, X_{T+n} = i_n, \dots, X_T = i_0\} = p_{i_n j} \, \mathbb{P}\{X_{T+n} = i_n, \dots, X_T = i_0\}.
$$

To this end note that

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb{P}\{X_{T+n+1} = j, X_{T+n} = i_n, \dots, X_T = i_0\} \\
&\quad = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}\{X_{T+n+1} = j, X_{T+n} = i_n, \dots, X_T = i_0, T = l\} \\
&\quad = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}\big( \{X_{l+n+1} = j\} \mid \{X_{l+n} = i_n, X_{l+n-1} = i_{n-1}, \dots, X_l = i_0, T = l\} \big) \\
&\qquad \cdot \mathbb{P}\{X_{l+n} = i_n, X_{l+n-1} = i_{n-1}, \dots, X_l = i_0, T = l\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here a term whose conditioning event has probability 0 is read as 0. Now, if ${n \ge 1,}$ the event ${\{X_{l+n-1} = i_{n-1}, \dots, X_l = i_0, T = l\}}$ belongs to the $\sigma$-algebra ${\mathcal{F}_{l+n-1},}$ and, by the usual Markov property, we find

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb{P}\{X_{T+n+1} = j, X_{T+n} = i_n, \dots, X_T = i_0\} \\
&\quad = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}\big( \{X_{l+n+1} = j\} \mid \{X_{l+n} = i_n\} \big) \\
&\qquad \cdot \mathbb{P}\{X_{l+n} = i_n, X_{l+n-1} = i_{n-1}, \dots, X_l = i_0, T = l\} \\
&\quad = p_{i_n j} \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}\{X_{l+n} = i_n, X_{l+n-1} = i_{n-1}, \dots, X_l = i_0, T = l\} \\
&\quad = p_{i_n j} \, \mathbb{P}\{X_{T+n} = i_n, X_{T+n-1} = i_{n-1}, \dots, X_T = i_0\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Indeed, ${\{T = l\} = \{T \le l\} \setminus \{T \le l - 1\}}$ belongs to $\mathcal{F}_l$ by Definition 6.2, and $\mathcal{F}_l$ is contained in ${\mathcal{F}_{l+n-1}.}$ Each event of $\mathcal{F}_{l+n-1}$ is a countable union of disjoint events ${\{X_{l+n-1} = k_{l+n-1}, \dots, X_0 = k_0\},}$ and condition (ii) of Definition 1.2 of the first post applies to each of them intersected with ${\{X_{l+n} = i_n\}.}$

The equality also holds for ${n = 0.}$ In detail, the event ${\{X_l = i_0, T = l\}}$ then belongs to $\mathcal{F}_l$ and is a countable union of disjoint events ${\{X_l = i_0, X_{l-1} = k_{l-1}, \dots, X_0 = k_0\},}$ to each of which condition (ii) applies. Dividing the equality of the first step by ${\mathbb{P}\{Y_n = i_n, \dots, Y_0 = i_0\} > 0}$ gives the first identity to check, since each event $\{Y_k = i_k\}$ differs from $\{X_{T+k} = i_k\}$ only inside the event that $T$ is infinite, of probability 0. The identity ${\mathbb{P}\{Y_{n+1} = j \mid Y_n = i_n\} = p_{i_n j}}$ can be checked in the same way. $\blacksquare$

### The stopped chain

We now prove that the Markov chain ${Y = (Y_m)_{m \ge 0}}$ is also independent of its past up to a stopping time $T$ with respect to the conditional probabilities ${\mathbb{P}\{\cdot \mid X_T = i, T < +\infty\}.}$ Figure 5 draws $Y$ and the chain stopped at time $T$ of Definition 6.5 on a path.

**Definition 6.5 (Stopped Markov chain).** Let $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ be a Markov chain and $T$ a stopping time of its natural filtration. The *Markov chain stopped at time* $T$ is the family $X^{|T}$ of random variables $(X^{|T}_n)_{n \ge 0}$ defined by ${X^{|T}_n = X_{T \wedge n},}$ where ${T \wedge n = \min\{T, n\}.}$

**Lemma 6.6.** Let $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ be a Markov chain. For every stopping time ${T,}$ all ${m, k \ge 0,}$ all ${i, j_1, \dots, j_m \in I}$ and every event $B$ belonging to the $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}_k$ with ${\mathbb{P}(\{X_k = i\} \cap \{T = k\} \cap B) > 0,}$ we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb{P}\big( \{X_{k+m} = j_m, \dots, X_{k+1} = j_1\} \mid \{X_k = i\} \cap \{T = k\} \cap B \big) \\
&\quad = \mathbb{P}\{X_{k+m} = j_m, \dots, X_{k+1} = j_1 \mid X_k = i\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Proof (complete).** The finite measures $\mathbb{Q}_1$ and $\mathbb{Q}_2$ on the $\sigma$-algebra ${\mathcal{F}_k,}$ defined for every ${B \in \mathcal{F}_k}$ by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{Q}_1(B) &= \mathbb{P}\big( \{X_{k+m} = j_m, \dots, X_{k+1} = j_1\} \cap \{X_k = i\} \cap \{T = k\} \cap B \big), \\
\mathbb{Q}_2(B) &= \mathbb{P}\{X_{k+m} = j_m, \dots, X_{k+1} = j_1 \mid X_k = i\} \cdot \mathbb{P}\big( \{X_k = i, T = k\} \cap B \big)
\end{aligned}
$$

coincide, because by the Markov property they coincide on the events of the form ${\{X_k = i_k, \dots, X_0 = i_0\},}$ which generate the $\sigma$-algebra ${\mathcal{F}_k.}$ In detail, as $\{X_k = i\} \cap \{T = k\}$ belongs to ${\mathcal{F}_k,}$ each such event $A$ is contained in it or disjoint from it, and in the first case ${\mathbb{Q}_1(A) = \mathbb{Q}_2(A)}$ is the Markov property, while in the second both vanish. Every event of $\mathcal{F}_k$ is a countable disjoint union of events ${A,}$ so ${\mathbb{Q}_1 = \mathbb{Q}_2,}$ and dividing by ${\mathbb{P}(\{X_k = i\} \cap \{T = k\} \cap B) > 0}$ gives the lemma. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 6.7.** Let $T$ and ${Y = (Y_m)_{m \ge 0}}$ be as in Theorem 6.4, and let $i$ be a state with ${\mathbb{P}\{X_T = i, T < +\infty\} > 0.}$ The families of random variables $Y$ and $X^{|T}$ are independent with respect to the probability measure ${\mathbb{P}\{\cdot \mid X_T = i, T < +\infty\}.}$

**Proof (complete).** Write ${j_0 = i.}$ It suffices to check that, for all ${n, m > 0}$ and all states ${j_1, \dots, j_m, i_0, \dots, i_n,}$ the events ${\{Y_m = j_m, \dots, Y_1 = j_1, Y_0 = j_0\}}$ and ${\{X_{T \wedge n} = i_n, \dots, X_{T \wedge 0} = i_0\}}$ are independent. We begin by computing

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb{P}\{Y_m = j_m, \dots, Y_0 = j_0, X_{T \wedge n} = i_n, \dots, X_0 = i_0 \mid X_T = i\} \\
&\quad = \sum_{k \ge 0} \mathbb{P}\{X_{k+m} = j_m, \dots, X_k = i, T = k, X_{k \wedge n} = i_n, \dots, X_0 = i_0\} / \mathbb{P}\{X_T = i\} \\
&\quad = \sum_{k \ge 0} \mathbb{P}\{X_{k+m} = j_m, \dots, X_{k+1} = j_1 \mid X_k = i, T = k, X_{k \wedge n} = i_n, \dots, X_0 = i_0\} \\
&\qquad \cdot \frac{\mathbb{P}\{X_k = i, T = k, X_{k \wedge n} = i_n, \dots, X_0 = i_0\}}{\mathbb{P}\{X_T = i\}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

By Lemma 6.6, for all ${k \ge 0,}$ we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb{P}\{X_{k+m} = j_m, \dots, X_{k+1} = j_1 \mid X_k = i, T = k, X_{k \wedge n} = i_n, \dots, X_0 = i_0\} \\
&\quad = \mathbb{P}\{X_{k+m} = j_m, \dots, X_{k+1} = j_1 \mid X_k = i\} \\
&\quad = \mathbb{P}\{Y_m = j_m, \dots, Y_1 = j_1 \mid X_T = i\}
\end{aligned}
$$

because $(Y_m)_{m \ge 0}$ is a Markov chain with the same transition matrix as $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ and ${\{X_T = i\} = \{Y_0 = i\} \cap \{T < +\infty\}.}$ Indeed, Lemma 6.6 applies with ${B = \{X_{k \wedge n} = i_n, \dots, X_0 = i_0\},}$ an event of $\mathcal{F}_k$ because ${k \wedge n \le k.}$ By (1.1) of the first post, the conditional probabilities given $\{X_k = i\}$ and given $\{X_T = i\}$ both equal ${p_{i j_1} p_{j_1 j_2} \cdots p_{j_{m-1} j_m},}$ as ${\mathbb{P}\{T < +\infty\} = 1.}$ We find then

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb{P}\{Y_m = j_m, \dots, Y_0 = j_0, X_{T \wedge n} = i_n, \dots, X_0 = i_0 \mid X_T = i\} \\
&\quad = \mathbb{P}\{Y_m = j_m, \dots, Y_1 = j_1 \mid X_T = i\} \\
&\qquad \cdot \sum_{k \ge 0} \frac{\mathbb{P}\{X_T = i, T = k, X_{T \wedge n} = i_n, \dots, X_0 = i_0\}}{\mathbb{P}\{X_T = i\}} \\
&\quad = \mathbb{P}\{Y_m = j_m, \dots, Y_1 = j_1 \mid X_T = i\} \cdot \mathbb{P}\{X_{T \wedge n} = i_n, \dots, X_0 = i_0 \mid X_T = i\}.
\end{aligned} \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

### Visits to a recurrent state

We can now prove that a Markov chain returns to a recurrent state $i$ infinitely many times.

**Theorem 6.8.** Let $i$ be a state, and let $N_i$ be the random variable with values in ${\mathbb{N} \cup \{+\infty\}}$

$$
N_i = \sum_{1 \le n < \infty} \mathbf{1}_{\{X_n = i\}},
$$

the number of visits in the state ${i.}$ The state $i$ is recurrent if and only if ${N_i = +\infty}$ almost surely under ${\mathbb{P}_i.}$

**Proof (complete).** Consider the sequence $(T_i^{(n)})_{n \ge 1}$ of stopping times inductively defined by

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_i^{(1)}(\omega) &= \min\{ n \ge 1 : X_n(\omega) = i \}, \\
T_i^{(n+1)}(\omega) &= \min\{ k > T_i^{(n)}(\omega) : X_k(\omega) = i \}
\end{aligned}
$$

(with ${\min \emptyset := +\infty}$). If $i$ is transient, the conclusion follows from Corollary 3.7, since ${\sum_{n \ge 0} p^{(n)}_{ii} < +\infty}$ for transient states. Indeed, by Corollary 3.7 the chain started at a transient state $i$ visits $i$ only finitely many times almost surely, so ${\mathbb{P}_i\{N_i = +\infty\} = 0}$ and both sides of the equivalence fail. Figure 6 draws the times $T_i^{(n)}$ on a path, together with the chain restarted at ${T_i^{(1)}.}$

If $i$ is recurrent, then, by Definition 3.1, the event $\{T_i^{(1)} < +\infty\}$ is $\mathbb{P}_i$-almost sure. It follows that, for all ${n > 0,}$ we have

$$
\mathbb{P}_i\{N_i \ge n\} = \mathbb{P}_i\{T_i^{(n)} < +\infty\} = \mathbb{P}_i\{T_i^{(n)} < +\infty \mid T_i^{(1)} < +\infty\}.
$$

Consider now the Markov chain $(Y_n)_{n \ge 0}$ restarted from ${T_i^{(1)}{:}}$

$$
Y_n(\omega) = \begin{cases} X_{T_i^{(1)}(\omega)+n}(\omega) & \text{if } T_i^{(1)}(\omega) < +\infty, \\ i_\infty & \text{if } T_i^{(1)}(\omega) = +\infty, \end{cases}
$$

where $i_\infty$ is an arbitrary state. Since the stopping time $T_i^{(1)}$ is $\mathbb{P}_i$-almost surely finite and the random variable $Y_0$ is $\mathbb{P}_i$-almost surely equal to ${i,}$ by Theorem 6.4 the sequences of random variables $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ and $(Y_n)_{n \ge 0}$ have the same distributions. Indeed, under $\mathbb{P}_i$ both are Markov chains with initial distribution $\delta_i$ and transition matrix ${(p_{ij})_{i, j \in I},}$ so by (1.1) of the first post their first $n$ terms have the same joint distribution for every ${n.}$

Moreover, the $n$-th entrance in $i$ of $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ coincides with the ${(n - 1)}$-th entrance in $i$ of ${(Y_n)_{n \ge 0},}$ as Figure 6 shows on a path. In detail, on $\{T_i^{(1)} < +\infty\}$ the time $m$ of $(Y_n)_{n \ge 0}$ is the time ${T_i^{(1)} + m}$ of ${(X_n)_{n \ge 0},}$ and each event ${\{T_i^{(n-1)} \le r\}}$ is decided by the first ${r + 1}$ terms of a sequence, whose joint distribution is the same for the two sequences.

Therefore we have ${\mathbb{P}_i\{T_i^{(n)} < +\infty \mid T_i^{(1)} < +\infty\} = \mathbb{P}_i\{T_i^{(n-1)} < +\infty\}}$ for ${n \ge 2.}$ Recursively, ${\mathbb{P}_i\{N_i \ge n\} = \dots = \mathbb{P}_i\{T_i^{(1)} < +\infty\} = 1}$ for all ${n,}$ that is, ${\mathbb{P}_i\{N_i = +\infty\} = 1.}$ $\blacksquare$
