A finite [Markov chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chain) is a matrix $P$ whose rows sum to one. Row $i$ is the distribution of the next state given that the current state is $i$. If $\mu_n$ is the row vector of probabilities over states at time $n$, then

$$
\mu_{n+1} = \mu_n P, \qquad \mu_n = \mu_0 P^n.
$$

That single line is the whole object. Simulating the chain means sampling from a row; analyzing it means multiplying by $P$ forever.

A stationary distribution is a fixed point of the multiplication:

$$
\pi P = \pi, \qquad \sum_i \pi_i = 1 .
$$

It is a left eigenvector of $P$ with eigenvalue $1$. The state of the chain does not stop moving when the distribution is stationary. The chain keeps jumping; what stays constant is the probability of finding it in each place.

The smallest example has two states. Let the chain leave state $1$ with probability $p$ and leave state $2$ with probability $q$:

$$
P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - p & p \\ q & 1 - q \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \pi = \left( \frac{q}{p+q},\ \frac{p}{p+q} \right).
$$

The check is one multiplication: $\pi_1 (1-p) + \pi_2 q = \frac{q(1-p) + pq}{p+q} = \frac{q}{p+q} = \pi_1$. The state that is harder to leave carries more of the mass. That reading survives in every larger chain I have seen so far.

Still open: Existence and uniqueness for infinite state spaces. The finite case hides how much the matrix is doing.
