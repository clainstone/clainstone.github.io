Does a Markov chain started at a state $i$ come back to $i$? The answer can be read from the $n$-step transition probabilities $p^{(n)}_{ii}$ of the previous posts.

## Recurrent and transient states

Throughout, $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ is a Markov chain on the state space $I$ with transition matrix ${P = (p_{ij}),}$ defined on a probability space with probability ${\mathbb{P}.}$

**Definition 3.1.** A state $i$ is called *recurrent* if

$$
\mathbb{P}\Big( \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} \{ X_n = i \} \,\Big|\, \{ X_0 = i \} \Big) = 1,
$$

otherwise it is called *transient*.

According to Definition 3.1, $i$ is recurrent if, starting from the state $i$ at time 0, the Markov chain returns to the state $i$ almost surely in a finite time. In particular, if ${p_{ii} = 1,}$ then $i$ is recurrent; such a state is called *absorbing*, or a *trap*.

### First entrance times

**Definition 3.2 (First entrance time).** Let $j$ be a state. The *first entrance time* in $j,$ or first visit time in $j,$ is the random variable with values in ${\mathbb{N} \cup \{+\infty\}}$

$$
T_j(\omega) = \begin{cases} \min\{ n \ge 1 : X_n(\omega) = j \} & \text{if } \{ n \ge 1 : X_n(\omega) = j \} \neq \emptyset, \\ +\infty & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

Note that, for every state $j,$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\{ T_j < +\infty \} &= \bigcup_{1 \le n < +\infty} \{ X_n = j \}, \\
\{ T_j = 1 \} &= \{ X_1 = j \}, \\
\{ T_j = n \} &= \{ X_n = j \} \cap \bigcap_{1 \le m \le n-1} \{ X_m \neq j \}, \qquad n > 1.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Notation.** For simplicity we denote by $\mathbb{P}_i$ the conditional probability

$$
\mathbb{P}_i(\cdot) = \mathbb{P}(\cdot \mid \{ X_0 = i \}),
$$

and by $\mathbb{E}_i$ the expectation with respect to ${\mathbb{P}_i.}$

For all ${n > 0}$ and all ${i, j \in I}$ let

$$
f^{(n)}_{ij} = \mathbb{P}_i\{ T_j = n \}, \qquad f^{(0)}_{ij} = 0,
$$

and also

$$
f^*_{ij} = \sum_{1 \le n < +\infty} f^{(n)}_{ij} = \mathbb{P}_i\{ T_j < +\infty \}.
$$

The Markov chain $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ is time-homogeneous, therefore, for every ${m \ge 0}$ with ${\mathbb{P}\{ X_m = i \} > 0,}$

$$
f^{(n)}_{ij} = \mathbb{P}\Big( \{ X_{m+n} = j \} \cap \bigcap_{1 \le \nu \le n-1} \{ X_{m+\nu} \neq j \} \,\Big|\, \{ X_m = i \} \Big).
$$

Indeed, conditional on ${X_m = i,}$ the chain $(X_{m+n})_{n \ge 0}$ is $\mathrm{Markov}(\delta_i, P)$ by the Markov property, so the event on the right has the probability that ${\{ T_j = n \}}$ has under ${\mathbb{P}_i.}$ Note that a state $i$ is recurrent if ${f^*_{ii} = 1}$ and transient if ${f^*_{ii} < 1.}$

### The renewal equation

**Theorem 3.3 (Renewal equation).** Let ${i, j \in I.}$ For all ${n \ge 1}$ we have

$$
p^{(n)}_{ij} = \sum_{\nu=1}^{n} f^{(\nu)}_{ij} p^{(n-\nu)}_{jj}. \tag{6}
$$

**Proof (complete).** Recall that ${\mathbb{P}_i = \mathbb{P}(\cdot \mid \{ X_0 = i \})}$ by definition. Since

$$
\{ X_n = j \} = \bigcup_{\nu=1}^{n} \big( \{ X_n = j \} \cap \{ T_j = \nu \} \big),
$$

by the Markov property

$$
\begin{aligned}
p^{(n)}_{ij} &= \mathbb{P}_i\{ X_n = j \} = \sum_{1 \le \nu \le n} \mathbb{P}_i\{ X_n = j, T_j = \nu \} \\
&= \mathbb{P}_i\{ X_n = j, X_{n-1} \neq j, \dots, X_2 \neq j, X_1 \neq j \} \\
&\quad + \sum_{1 \le \nu \le n-1} \mathbb{P}_i\{ X_n = j, X_\nu = j, X_{\nu-1} \neq j, \dots, X_1 \neq j \}
\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
p^{(n)}_{ij} &= f^{(n)}_{ij} + \sum_{1 \le \nu \le n-1} \mathbb{P}_i\{ X_n = j \mid X_\nu = j, X_{\nu-1} \neq j, \dots, X_1 \neq j \} \\
&\qquad \cdot \mathbb{P}_i\{ X_\nu = j, X_{\nu-1} \neq j, \dots, X_1 \neq j \} \\
&= f^{(n)}_{ij} + \sum_{1 \le \nu \le n-1} \mathbb{P}_i\{ X_n = j \mid X_\nu = j \} \, \mathbb{P}_i\{ T_j = \nu \} \\
&= f^{(n)}_{ij} + \sum_{1 \le \nu \le n-1} f^{(\nu)}_{ij} p^{(n-\nu)}_{jj}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This proves (6). Indeed, the event ${\{ X_{\nu-1} \neq j, \dots, X_1 \neq j \}}$ is determined by ${X_0, \dots, X_\nu,}$ so the Markov property removes it from the condition, and $f^{(n)}_{ij}$ is the term ${\nu = n}$ of (6) because ${p^{(0)}_{jj} = 1.}$ $\blacksquare$

### A criterion for recurrence

The proof of Theorem 3.5 uses Lemma 3.4, also known as [Abel's lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel%27s_theorem). It could be proved directly, but it is an immediate consequence of the [monotone convergence theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotone_convergence_theorem), and its proof is skipped.

**Definition (Radius of convergence).** Let $(a_n)_{n \ge 0}$ be a sequence of real numbers. The *radius of convergence* of the power series ${\sum_{n \ge 0} a_n s^n}$ is

$$
r = \sup \Big\{ \rho \ge 0 : \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} |a_n| \rho^n < +\infty \Big\} \in [0, +\infty].
$$

For every real $s$ with ${|s| < r}$ the series converges absolutely. By the definition of the supremum there is ${\rho > |s|}$ with ${\sum_n |a_n| \rho^n < +\infty,}$ and ${|a_n s^n| \le |a_n| \rho^n}$ for every $n.$ For ${|s| > r}$ the series diverges.

**Lemma 3.4.** Let $(a_n)_{n \ge 0}$ be a sequence of nonnegative real numbers such that the power series

$$
A(s) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n s^n
$$

has radius of convergence ${r \ge 1.}$ Then, with both sides possibly equal to ${+\infty,}$

$$
\lim_{s \to 1^-} A(s) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n.
$$

**Theorem 3.5.** For every state $i$ the following are equivalent.\
**(a)** The state $i$ is recurrent.\
**(b)** The series $\sum_{n \ge 0} p^{(n)}_{ii}$ is divergent.

If $i$ is transient, then

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} p^{(n)}_{ii} = \frac{1}{1 - f^*_{ii}} = \frac{1}{\mathbb{P}_i\{ T_i = \infty \}}.
$$

**Proof (complete).** Consider the generating functions of the sequences $(p^{(n)}_{ii})_{n \ge 0}$ and ${(f^{(n)}_{ii})_{n \ge 0},}$

$$
P_{ii}(s) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} p^{(n)}_{ii} s^n, \qquad F_{ii}(s) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f^{(n)}_{ii} s^n.
$$

In both cases the radius of convergence is ${r \ge 1,}$ because the sequences $(p^{(n)}_{ii})_{n \ge 0}$ and $(f^{(n)}_{ii})_{n \ge 0}$ are bounded. In detail, every term is a probability, so ${0 \le p^{(n)}_{ii} \le 1}$ and ${0 \le f^{(n)}_{ii} \le 1}$ for all $n.$ Hence, for every ${\rho \in [0, 1),}$ the geometric series bounds both series of absolute values:

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} p^{(n)}_{ii} \rho^n \le \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \rho^n = \frac{1}{1 - \rho} < +\infty, \qquad \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f^{(n)}_{ii} \rho^n \le \frac{1}{1 - \rho} < +\infty.
$$

Every ${\rho \in [0, 1)}$ therefore belongs to the set whose supremum is the radius of convergence, and that supremum is at least 1. In particular both series converge absolutely for every ${s \in (-1, 1),}$ the range of $s$ used in the rest of the proof. By the renewal equation (6) of Theorem 3.3 with ${j = i,}$ for all ${n \ge 1}$ we have

$$
p^{(n)}_{ii} s^n = s^n \sum_{\nu=1}^{n} f^{(\nu)}_{ii} p^{(n-\nu)}_{ii} = \sum_{\nu=1}^{n} f^{(\nu)}_{ii} s^\nu p^{(n-\nu)}_{ii} s^{n-\nu}.
$$

The second equality brings $s^n$ inside the sum and writes it as ${s^n = s^\nu s^{n-\nu}.}$ Summing over ${n \ge 1,}$ we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{ii}(s) - 1 &= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} p^{(n)}_{ii} s^n \\
&= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sum_{\nu=1}^{n} f^{(\nu)}_{ii} s^\nu p^{(n-\nu)}_{ii} s^{n-\nu} \\
&= \sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty} f^{(\nu)}_{ii} s^\nu \sum_{n=\nu}^{\infty} p^{(n-\nu)}_{ii} s^{n-\nu} \\
&= \sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty} f^{(\nu)}_{ii} s^\nu \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} p^{(n)}_{ii} s^n \\
&= F_{ii}(s) P_{ii}(s).
\end{aligned}
$$

The first equality holds because ${p^{(0)}_{ii} = \mathbb{P}_i\{ X_0 = i \} = 1,}$ so the term ${n = 0}$ of $P_{ii}(s)$ is 1. The second replaces each $p^{(n)}_{ii} s^n$ with the previous display. The third exchanges the order of summation: the pairs ${(n, \nu)}$ with ${1 \le \nu \le n}$ are exactly the pairs with ${\nu \ge 1}$ and ${n \ge \nu.}$ The fourth renames ${n - \nu}$ as $n$ in the inner sum, which then runs from 0 and no longer depends on $\nu.$ The fifth takes out the common factor $P_{ii}(s)$ and uses ${f^{(0)}_{ii} = 0,}$ so that the sum over ${\nu \ge 1}$ is ${F_{ii}(s).}$

The exchange in the third equality is allowed because the double series converges absolutely for ${|s| < 1.}$ Indeed, the numbers $f^{(\nu)}_{ii}$ and $p^{(m)}_{ii}$ are nonnegative, so replacing $s$ with $|s|$ replaces each term with its absolute value. By the first two equalities, applied at $|s|,$ the double series of absolute values sums to ${P_{ii}(|s|) - 1,}$ which is finite because ${|s| < 1 \le r.}$ A double series whose absolute values have a finite sum can be summed in either order, with the same result.

The chain of equalities reads ${P_{ii}(s) - 1 = F_{ii}(s) P_{ii}(s),}$ that is ${P_{ii}(s) (1 - F_{ii}(s)) = 1.}$ To divide by ${1 - F_{ii}(s)}$ it must be nonzero. Since ${f^{(0)}_{ii} = 0}$ and ${|s|^n \le |s|}$ for ${n \ge 1,}$

$$
|F_{ii}(s)| \le \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f^{(n)}_{ii} |s|^n \le |s| \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f^{(n)}_{ii} = |s| f^*_{ii} \le |s| < 1,
$$

where ${f^*_{ii} \le 1}$ because it is a probability. Hence ${1 - F_{ii}(s) > 0,}$ and therefore, for all ${s \in (-1, 1),}$

$$
P_{ii}(s) = \frac{1}{1 - F_{ii}(s)}.
$$

The conclusion follows from an application of Abel's lemma, noting that

$$
\lim_{s \to 1^-} F_{ii}(s) = f^*_{ii}, \qquad \lim_{s \to 1^-} P_{ii}(s) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} p^{(n)}_{ii}.
$$

Both limits follow from Lemma 3.4, applied to ${a_n = f^{(n)}_{ii}}$ and to ${a_n = p^{(n)}_{ii}{:}}$ the terms are nonnegative, and the radius of convergence is at least 1 by the first step of the proof. In the first limit the sum of the series is ${\sum_{n \ge 1} f^{(n)}_{ii} = f^*_{ii},}$ because ${f^{(0)}_{ii} = 0;}$ the second limit may be ${+\infty.}$

Now let ${s \to 1^-}$ in ${P_{ii}(s) = 1/(1 - F_{ii}(s)).}$ Recall that $i$ is recurrent if ${f^*_{ii} = 1}$ and transient if ${f^*_{ii} < 1.}$ If $i$ is recurrent, then ${1 - F_{ii}(s)}$ is positive and tends to 0, so $P_{ii}(s)$ tends to ${+\infty;}$ by the second limit the series $\sum_{n \ge 0} p^{(n)}_{ii}$ diverges. If $i$ is transient, then ${1 - F_{ii}(s)}$ tends to ${1 - f^*_{ii} > 0,}$ so ${\sum_{n \ge 0} p^{(n)}_{ii} = 1/(1 - f^*_{ii}) < +\infty.}$ The first case shows that (a) implies (b); the second shows that if (a) fails then (b) fails, that is, (b) implies (a). Finally, since ${\{ T_i = \infty \}}$ is the complement of ${\{ T_i < +\infty \},}$ we have ${\mathbb{P}_i\{ T_i = \infty \} = 1 - f^*_{ii},}$ which gives the formula for transient $i.$ $\blacksquare$

### Classes and visits

**Corollary 3.6.** Let $J$ be a class of states and ${j \in J.}$ If $j$ is recurrent (respectively, transient), then all elements of $J$ are recurrent (respectively, transient).

**Proof (complete).** Let ${k \in J \setminus \{j\}.}$ Since $k$ and $j$ communicate, there exist ${l, m \ge 1}$ such that

$$
p^{(l)}_{jk} > 0, \qquad p^{(m)}_{kj} > 0.
$$

For every integer ${n \ge 1}$ we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
p^{(l+n+m)}_{kk} &\ge p^{(m)}_{kj} p^{(n)}_{jj} p^{(l)}_{jk}, \\
p^{(l+n+m)}_{jj} &\ge p^{(l)}_{jk} p^{(n)}_{kk} p^{(m)}_{kj},
\end{aligned}
$$

so that the series $\sum_{n \ge 0} p^{(n)}_{kk}$ and $\sum_{n \ge 0} p^{(n)}_{jj}$ are both convergent or both divergent. The conclusion follows from Theorem 3.5. $\blacksquare$

For every state ${j,}$ the sum of the series $\sum_{n \ge 0} p^{(n)}_{ij}$ is the average sojourn time in $j,$ the average number of visits to ${j,}$ starting from $i.$ The random variable $\sum_{n \ge 0} \mathbf{1}_{\{ X_n = j \}}$ represents the random time spent in ${j,}$ because it counts how many times the event ${\{ X_n = j \}}$ occurs, and its expectation is

$$
\mathbb{E}_i\Big[ \sum_{n \ge 0} \mathbf{1}_{\{ X_n = j \}} \Big] = \sum_{n \ge 0} \mathbb{E}_i\big[ \mathbf{1}_{\{ X_n = j \}} \big] = \sum_{n \ge 0} \mathbb{P}_i\{ X_n = j \} = \sum_{n \ge 0} p^{(n)}_{ij}. \tag{7}
$$

**Corollary 3.7.** If $j$ is a transient state, then for every state $i$ the series

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} p^{(n)}_{ij} \tag{8}
$$

is convergent. In particular, starting from any state ${i,}$ the Markov chain visits $j$ only a finite number of times almost surely.

**Proof (complete).** If ${i = j,}$ convergence follows from Theorem 3.5. If ${i \neq j,}$ then, by the renewal equation ${(6),}$ we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} p^{(n)}_{ij} &= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sum_{\nu=1}^{n} f^{(\nu)}_{ij} p^{(n-\nu)}_{jj} \\
&= \sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty} f^{(\nu)}_{ij} \sum_{n=\nu}^{\infty} p^{(n-\nu)}_{jj} \\
&= f^*_{ij} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} p^{(n)}_{jj} < \infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

In particular, by ${(7),}$ the random variable $\sum_{n \ge 0} \mathbf{1}_{\{ X_n = j \}}$ has finite expectation under ${\mathbb{P}_i.}$ It follows that it is almost surely finite, and so only a finite number of the events ${\{ X_n = j \}}$ occur. Indeed, the sums can be exchanged because their terms are nonnegative, the last series converges by Theorem 3.5 because $j$ is transient, and ${p^{(0)}_{ij} = 0}$ for ${i \neq j.}$ $\blacksquare$

In the case where the number of states is finite, the Markov chain cannot visit only transient states, each one a finite number of times. Corollary 3.8 is a consequence.

**Corollary 3.8.** A Markov chain with a finite set of states $I$ has at least one recurrent state.

**Proof (complete).** Suppose, by contradiction, that all states are transient. For all ${i, j}$ the series (8) is convergent; in particular

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} p^{(n)}_{ij} = 0.
$$

The matrix $P^{(n)}$ is stochastic, and so, for all ${n \ge 1,}$ we also have

$$
\sum_{j \in I} p^{(n)}_{ij} = 1.
$$

Taking the limit as $n$ goes to infinity and exchanging limit and sum on ${j,}$ we find the contradiction ${0 = 1.}$ $\blacksquare$
