How do the $n$-step transition probabilities $p^{(n)}_{ij}$ of a recurrent Markov chain behave as ${n \to \infty,}$ and what do their limits have to do with the invariant densities of the previous post?

## Limit theorems

Let $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ be a Markov chain on the probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ with set of states $I$ and transition matrix ${P = (p_{ij})_{i, j \in I}.}$ As in the previous posts, $\mathbb{P}_i$ and $\mathbb{E}_i$ are the probability and the expectation given ${X_0 = i,}$ ${p^{(n)}_{ij} = \mathbb{P}_i\{X_n = j\},}$ and $T_j$ is the first entrance time in $j$ of Definition 3.2, with ${f^{(n)}_{ij} = \mathbb{P}_i\{T_j = n\}.}$ An invariant density is a probability density $(\pi_j)_{j \in I}$ such that ${\pi = \pi P,}$ as in Definition 5.1 and Proposition 5.2 of the previous post. Theorem 5.4 is stated without proof; its most common proofs are based either on analytical methods or on the law of large numbers.

**Theorem 5.4.** Let $i$ be a recurrent aperiodic state. Then, with the convention ${(+\infty)^{-1} = 0,}$

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} p^{(n)}_{ii} = (\mathbb{E}_i[T_i])^{-1}.
$$

**Definition 5.5 (Fast and slow recurrent states).** A recurrent state $i$ is called *fast recurrent*, or *positive recurrent*, if ${\mathbb{E}_i[T_i] < +\infty,}$ and *slow recurrent*, or *null recurrent*, if ${\mathbb{E}_i[T_i] = +\infty.}$

If $i$ and $j$ are communicating recurrent and aperiodic states, then, in the sense of Definition 5.5, they are both fast recurrent or both slow recurrent. It suffices to note that we have inequalities like

$$
p^{(a+n+b)}_{jj} \ge p^{(a)}_{ji} p^{(n)}_{ii} p^{(b)}_{ij},
$$

where ${a, b \ge 1}$ are chosen in such a way that ${p^{(a)}_{ji} > 0}$ and ${p^{(b)}_{ij} > 0,}$ and to apply Theorem 5.4.

One can also check that a finite-state Markov chain has no slow recurrent states. Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a class with a slow recurrent state ${i.}$ By the argument above, all states in $\mathcal{C}$ are slow recurrent, so that the sequence $(p^{(n)}_{jj})_{n \ge 1}$ converges to 0 as $n$ goes to $+\infty$ for all ${j \in \mathcal{C}.}$ In detail, the states of $\mathcal{C}$ are recurrent by Corollary 3.6, and the argument above rests on Theorem 5.4, so the reasoning here covers a class of aperiodic states, for which ${\lim_{n} p^{(n)}_{jj} = (\mathbb{E}_j[T_j])^{-1} = 0.}$

Moreover, since it is not possible to exit from $\mathcal{C}$ without leaving it forever, and in this case $i$ would not be recurrent, we have

$$
\sum_{j \in \mathcal{C}} p^{(n)}_{kj} = 1
$$

for every state ${k \in \mathcal{C}}$ and all ${n.}$ Taking the limit as $n$ goes to $+\infty$ we get a contradiction.

To see this, fix ${k \in \mathcal{C}}$ and ${j \in \mathcal{C}{:}}$ by the renewal equation of Theorem 3.3, $p^{(n)}_{kj}$ is the sum over ${1 \le \nu \le n}$ of the terms ${f^{(\nu)}_{kj} p^{(n-\nu)}_{jj},}$ each of which tends to 0 and is at most ${f^{(\nu)}_{kj}.}$ Since ${\sum_{\nu} f^{(\nu)}_{kj} \le 1,}$ the [dominated convergence theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominated_convergence_theorem) gives ${p^{(n)}_{kj} \to 0,}$ so the finite sum over $j$ tends to 0 and not to 1.

**Theorem 5.6.** Let $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ be an irreducible aperiodic Markov chain. The following are equivalent.\
**(1)** All states are fast recurrent.

**(2)** There exists an invariant density $(\pi_i)_{i \in I}$ such that, for all states $i$ and ${j,}$ we have

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} p^{(n)}_{ij} = \lim_{n \to \infty} p^{(n)}_{jj} = \pi_j.
$$

In the case where the conditions (1) and (2) hold, $(\pi_i)_{i \in I}$ is the unique invariant density for the Markov chain ${(X_n)_{n \ge 0}.}$

**Proof (complete).** First, (1) implies (2). By Theorem 3.3, the renewal equation, we have

$$
p^{(n)}_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} f^{(k)}_{ij} p^{(n-k)}_{jj},
$$

therefore, by Theorem 5.4 and dominated convergence, calling $\pi_j$ the limit of the sequence ${(p^{(n)}_{jj})_{n \ge 0},}$ one finds

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} p^{(n)}_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} f^{(k)}_{ij} \pi_j = \pi_j.
$$

Indeed, the second equality holds because ${\sum_{k \ge 1} f^{(k)}_{ij} = \mathbb{P}_i\{T_j < +\infty\} = 1}$ in an irreducible chain whose states are recurrent.

Moreover, for all finite subsets $F$ of ${I,}$ we have the inequality

$$
\sum_{j \in F} \pi_j = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{j \in F} p^{(n)}_{ij} \le \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{j \in I} p^{(n)}_{ij} = 1,
$$

so that ${\sum_{j \in I} \pi_j \le 1.}$ We now prove that $(\pi_j)_{j \in I}$ is an invariant density for the Markov chain ${(X_n)_{n \ge 0}.}$ Let $F$ be a finite subset of ${I.}$ Taking the limit as ${n \to +\infty}$ in

$$
\sum_{k \in F} p^{(n)}_{ik} p_{kj} \le p^{(n+1)}_{ij}
$$

for all ${i, j \in I,}$ we find the inequalities ${\sum_{k \in F} \pi_k p_{kj} \le \pi_j}$ and so, by the arbitrariness of ${F,}$

$$
\sum_{k \in I} \pi_k p_{kj} \le \pi_j. \tag{11}
$$

Summing on $j$ we also find

$$
\sum_{k \in I} \pi_k = \sum_{j \in I} \sum_{k \in I} \pi_k p_{kj} = \sum_{j \in I} \pi_j
$$

and the inequalities (11) must be equalities.

Iterating the equalities (11) we find the identity ${\pi_j = \sum_{k \in I} \pi_k p^{(n)}_{kj}}$ for all ${n \ge 1.}$ Taking the limit as $n$ goes to infinity, we have

$$
\pi_j = \pi_j \sum_{k \in I} \pi_k
$$

for every state ${j,}$ so that, since ${\pi_j > 0}$ because $j$ is fast recurrent, ${\sum_{k \in I} \pi_k = 1.}$ Therefore $(\pi_j)_{j \in I}$ is a probability density which is invariant for ${(X_n)_{n \ge 0}.}$

Next, (2) implies (1). This follows from Theorem 5.4. Indeed, if a state were transient, all states would be, by Corollary 3.6, and Theorem 3.5 would give ${p^{(n)}_{jj} \to 0}$ for every ${j,}$ against ${\sum_j \pi_j = 1;}$ so all states are recurrent, and aperiodic by hypothesis. Since the $\pi_j$ sum to ${1,}$ some ${\pi_j = (\mathbb{E}_j[T_j])^{-1}}$ is positive by Theorem 5.4, so $j$ is fast recurrent, and then so is every state, by the argument after Definition 5.5.

We finally show that, if (1) and (2) hold, then the invariant density is unique. Note that, if $\mu$ is another invariant density, then, for every state ${j,}$ we have ${\mu_j = \sum_{k \in I} \mu_k p_{kj}.}$ Iterating $n$ times we find the identity ${\mu_j = \sum_{k \in I} \mu_k p^{(n)}_{kj}.}$ Taking the limit as $n$ goes to infinity,

$$
\mu_j = \sum_{k \in I} \mu_k \pi_j = \pi_j \quad \text{for all } j \in I. \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$
