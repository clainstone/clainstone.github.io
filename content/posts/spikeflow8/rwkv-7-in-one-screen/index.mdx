RWKV-7 replaces attention with a recurrent state. For each head the state $S_t$ is a small square matrix, and at every token it is updated in place. Written schematically, one head does

$$
S_t \;=\; S_{t-1}\,\big(\operatorname{diag}(w_t) \;-\; \hat\kappa_t\,(a_t \odot \hat\kappa_t)^{\!\top}\big) \;+\; v_t\, k_t^{\!\top},
\qquad
o_t \;=\; S_t\, r_t .
$$

Three things happen in that line.

**Decay.** $\operatorname{diag}(w_t)$ scales each channel of the state by a data-dependent factor. Old information fades at a rate the model chooses per token.

**Correction.** The rank-one term removes part of the state along the direction $\hat\kappa_t$, with strength set by $a_t$. The paper calls $a_t$ an in-context learning rate. It lets the model overwrite what it stored earlier rather than only letting it fade.

**Write.** $v_t k_t^\top$ adds the new key–value pair.

The read is a matrix–vector product with $r_t$. Everything else in the block, from the token shift to the projections that produce $w_t, k_t, v_t, r_t, a_t$ and $\hat\kappa_t$, is ordinary dense computation.

For an engine this is good news and a constraint at once. Good news: the per-token cost is fixed, there is no growing cache, and the state fits in a known amount of memory. Constraint: the update is a sequence of small matrix operations on a per-head state, and the accelerator has to be fed that shape efficiently.

The formulas here follow the RWKV-7 paper by the RWKV team. My contribution is the engine, not the model.

Still open: Which of the per-token vectors are cheap enough to compute on the NPU and which should stay on the host. That decision is what the first measurement has to inform.
